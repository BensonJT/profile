How to See the Forest
and the Trees

The Combination

Jeffrey Benson  ·  2025

Forest & Trees Series  ·  Part I  |  Part II  |  Part III  |  Part IV  |  Part V  |  Part VI
Originally published on LinkedIn — View or comment there →

To see the forest and the trees, you develop the capability to step back and see the big picture, while simultaneously being able to zoom in and observe the trees. This series of articles will teach some practical things that will help you to cultivate this skill.

In Part I we introduced the Context Diagram and the ICOMs that define any process boundary. In Part II we decomposed that context into an Activity Node Tree. In Part III we applied ICOMs to every node in the tree. In Part IV we added SIPOC, listing the suppliers and customers at the boundaries of the process.

On the asset lifecycle I described in Part I, the model lived in a spreadsheet of 400 rows, one for each activity, each with its Activity ID. In 2014, a year after the SIPOC and ICOM tree-node model from Part IV, this combined model (the functional decomposition process map) was nominated and presented at Verizon's Lean Six Sigma Best Practice Conference.

We now have two frameworks. ICOM tells us what flows into each activity, what governs it, what it produces, and what enables it. SIPOC tells us who supplies each activity and who receives what it produces. In Part IV we asked what happens when you apply SIPOC at every activity node in the decomposition tree, and not only at the top level. This part answers that question.

Applying SIPOC at Every Node

In Part IV, we built a single SIPOC for the Baking Brownies process. It listed the suppliers and the customers of the process, but it treated the process as a black box: the activities inside had no supplier-customer relationships of their own.

When we apply SIPOC at every activity node, the same way we applied ICOMs in Part III, we find that every activity has its own suppliers and its own customers.

In this model, the Supplier of an activity is first the role performing the activity, supplying their time, skill, and judgment to turn inputs into outputs. Suppliers of materials (a grocery store, a utility company) are listed with them because they provide the inputs.

Take A3 (Bake & Serve Brownies). Its Suppliers are the Baker and the Oven Operator performing A3. The inputs they receive (brownie batter and greased pan) were produced by A2, and the mechanism they depend on (a preheated oven) came from A1. Those deliveries from upstream show up in A3's Inputs and Mechanisms columns.

And the Supplier of A2 (Mix Ingredients) is the Baker performing A2, the person doing the mixing. Their Customer is the Baker performing A3, who depends on receiving a properly prepared batter. If A2 delivers a poorly mixed batter, the model shows where the failure occurred and who was accountable for it.

In the brownie example, the Baker performs most of the steps. In a business process, those are usually different people or teams, and each handoff is an accountability boundary. That boundary is invisible in an L1 SIPOC, and it becomes visible when you apply SIPOC at every node.

One more case: when an activity is fully automated (a script, a programmed workflow, or an AI agent performs it with no human involved), the system is still a Mechanism, and we also name it as the Supplier because it performs the activity. When a person does the work with the help of a system, the system stays a Mechanism and the person's role is the Supplier.

Combining SIPOC and ICOM at every node lets you trace every input and output back to the role (or system) that supplied it and forward to the role that receives it, at every level of the process.

A Refinement When Combining IDEF0 with SIPOC

When you bring SIPOC and ICOM together at every activity node, the Mechanisms category needs a refinement.

In Parts I through III, Mechanisms were defined as they are in standard IDEF0: the people, tools, and systems that enable an activity to execute. The Baker, the oven, and the mixing bowl are all Mechanisms.

That definition is correct in a pure IDEF0 context, and Parts I through III were built on it.

But once SIPOC is in the picture, leaving people in the Mechanisms category creates a conflict. SIPOC already has a place for people: Supplier (who provides the inputs or performs the activity) and Customer (who receives the outputs, including the final customers the process serves). If the Baker stays a Mechanism, the same person appears twice, in two categories, for two purposes.

So the combined model makes one change:

Mechanisms = Tools and Systems only.

People move to Supplier (the person or team who provides the inputs or performs the activity) and Customer (the person or team who receives). A system that fully automates a step stays a Mechanism and is also named as the Supplier.

Parts I through III presented the ICOMs as IDEF0 defines them, and that is still true when you use IDEF0 alone. This refinement applies when SIPOC and ICOM are combined, so that each person has one place in the model.

Under this refinement, the Baker is:

  • A Supplier who provides their skill, time, and attention to the activity
  • A Customer who receives the outputs of prior activities and depends on them being right

The oven, the mixing bowl, the timer, and the cooling rack remain Mechanisms. They enable the activity, but they neither supply it nor receive from it.

The Combined Model: A2 Mix Ingredients

Let's apply the combined model (SIPOC + ICOM with the Mechanisms refinement) to A2 (Mix Ingredients). A2 has the most elements of any activity in the brownie process, so it makes a good example.

Supplier / Role: Baker + Grocery Store Clerk

Who is supplying the inputs? Who is performing the activity?

The Supplier for any activity captures two things: who provides the inputs, and who performs the work.

The Grocery Store Clerk and Baker supply the physical ingredients (brownie mix, eggs, water, and grease). They deliver the raw materials that A2 will consume. If the grocery store is out of eggs, A2 cannot begin.

The Baker supplies the labor, skill, and judgment to do the mixing, because someone has to combine the ingredients in the right proportions and the right sequence. The one performing the activity is also a Supplier.

Both are listed: one delivers the materials and the other performs the work.

Inputs: Brownie Mix, Eggs, Water, Grease

What comes into this activity that is transformed or consumed?

These are the materials that A2 consumes and transforms, the same Inputs we identified with the ICOMs in Part III. SIPOC adds who is responsible for delivering them.

Controls: Recipe, Customer Preference

What outside force constrains the process?

The recipe governs how much of each ingredient to use, in what order, and how to combine them. Customer Preference governs the variations (more chocolate, less sugar, brownies or blondies). Controls are not consumed. They govern the activity, and they come from standards, preferences, and operating norms.

Outputs: Brownie Batter, Trash, Greased Pan

What is the output from this activity? What does the supplier deliver?

A2 produces three outputs. The brownie batter is the primary output, the transformed result of combining all inputs. Trash (egg shells, packaging) is an incidental output that must be managed. The greased pan is a prepared output that flows directly to A3 alongside the batter. Each of the three outputs has a destination. The pan itself is a Mechanism. Greasing it changes it, so the Greased Pan leaves A2 as an output and flows to A3 with the batter.

Mechanisms: Mixing Bowl & Spoon, Pan

What systems or tools are required to turn the inputs into outputs?

Under the combined model, only tools and systems remain in Mechanisms. The mixing bowl, spoon, and pan enable the mixing without being consumed, and the Baker who uses them is the Supplier.

Customer: Baker (performing A3)

Who is the output delivered to? Who is the activity performed for?

The immediate customer of A2's output is the Baker who will execute A3 (Bake & Serve Brownies). They depend on A2 delivering a correctly prepared batter: the right consistency, right quantity, in a properly prepared pan. If A2 delivers the wrong consistency, the wrong amount, or an ungreased pan, A3 will fail even if everything else goes right.

Here the Baker performs both A2 and A3. In a business process the next activity is often performed by a different person or team, and naming them makes the handoff visible. A standard L1 SIPOC cannot see that internal supplier-customer relationship.

Each activity now has a named Supplier (the role performing it), a named Customer (the role depending on its output), and its inputs, outputs, controls, and mechanisms. A4 (Cleanup) is the kind of activity that often goes undocumented, even though it has inputs, outputs, people responsible for it, and a customer (the Baker, who needs clean equipment for the next batch). Leave an activity like this off the model, and you leave its accountability off too.

If you are troubleshooting a bad batch of brownies, you can now work through the model: which Supplier failed to deliver? Which input was wrong? Which control was violated? Which mechanism was unavailable?

The Spreadsheet: One Row Per Activity

The combined SIPOC + ICOM model becomes something a team can work with when you put it in a spreadsheet.

Each row in the spreadsheet represents one activity, and each column captures one element of the model, so every activity, dependency, and accountability relationship in the process is visible in one view.

How Deep Do You Go?

Every process model should have at minimum three levels of decomposition:

  • L0 - the Context Diagram: the entire process as a single box, with its external inputs, controls, outputs, and mechanisms
  • L1 - the major activities inside that box (Preheat Oven, Mix Ingredients, Bake & Serve Brownies, Cleanup)
  • L2 - the sub-activities within each L1 step (Secure Fuel, Check Oven, Ignite Oven…)

Whether you need L3 or deeper depends on the scope. For a small, local process like baking brownies, L2 is enough. For end-to-end business processes that cross departments, systems, or organizations, you will need L3 and sometimes L4 or beyond (the asset lifecycle went to L6 in places). Decompose down to the level where the work can be analyzed, assigned, and improved.

The spreadsheet handles this through the Activity IDs, which encode the level: A1 is L1, A1.1 is L2, and A1.1.1 would be L3. Every row, at every level, has the same nine columns.

The Critical KPIs and Problems / Pain Points columns extend the model beyond IDEF0 and SIPOC. Once you know what each activity produces and who depends on it, you can ask how well it performs and where it fails. These two columns connect the model to how the business runs day to day.

Because the model is a data table, it can be filtered, sorted, analyzed, and extended in ways a diagram cannot. Every row is a unit of work (an activity within a process, in its context), and every column is a question to ask about it.

A process that lives in this spreadsheet can be diagnosed, discussed, and improved by any team, without requiring a Visio license, an IDEF0 tool, or a Lean Six Sigma certification (although, the Lean Six Sigma certification is a PLUS!).

Why This Matters

A traditional IDEF0 practitioner applies ICOMs at every node and produces a decomposition diagram. A traditional Lean Six Sigma practitioner builds a SIPOC and produces a stakeholder map. Each is useful, and each leaves something out.

The IDEF0 decomposition can tell you that a brownie-mixing activity needs a recipe and a bowl, but it cannot tell you who is responsible for keeping the recipe current, or who gets called when the batter is wrong.

The SIPOC stakeholder map can tell you that the Grocery Store Clerk is a supplier and the Family is a customer, but it cannot tell you which activities inside the process are failing and why.

The combined model, applied at every node, answers both questions and keeps the structure, the governance, and the accountability of the process in one spreadsheet. That is how we see the forest and the trees: every activity is a row, and every row rolls up through its Activity ID to A0.

In Summary

Applying SIPOC at every activity node shows the internal supplier-customer relationships that a traditional L1 SIPOC cannot see. Combined with ICOM at every node, it gives each activity a complete profile: who supplies it, what flows in, what governs it, what it produces, what enables it, and who depends on the output.

The Mechanisms refinement puts people in Supplier and Customer and leaves tools and systems in Mechanisms, so each person has one place in the model (a system that fully automates a step is also named as its Supplier).

The spreadsheet holds the model: one row per activity at every level of decomposition, with Critical KPIs and Problems added. L1 gives you the high-level picture, L2 the work, and L3 and beyond the detail a large end-to-end process requires. Any team can use it without specialized software.

In Part VI we will add Value Stream Analysis. Each activity can be measured (cycle time, value-added time, wait time, defect rate), and from those numbers we can calculate two metrics that tell you how well the process performs and where the waste is.
