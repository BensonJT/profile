Six Practices of Servant Leadership

Jeffrey Benson  ·  May 2026

In 1999 an SVP mandated ISO 9000 across his entire organization, and nobody in it knew the standard, including me. A leader who had inherited fifty or more database, server and storage engineers running production around the clock asked me to help her implement it. Morale was already low, half her team left every year, and nobody could say exactly why. I was new to ISO, new to 24x7 production support, and young.

That assignment taught me something I have seen many times since. Most leaders already agree with the principles of servant leadership (empathy, transparency, investing in their people's growth), and the principles hold up until a quarter closes badly, a project falls behind, or a stakeholder relationship gets complicated. Then the first casualty is the framework they said they believed in.

What was missing was the practice: habits that hold up under pressure.

Over three decades in high-pressure operational environments (workforce planning, enterprise transformation, quality systems), six operating principles have held up for me as practices that worked when everything else was under pressure. I didn't invent any of them, and each one came out of the work with a story behind it.

01

Lead with Empathy

Understand what someone is dealing with before judging their performance. Context precedes prescription.

The surface diagnosis for that 24x7 team was competence, and I almost missed the weight they were carrying. The issue was clarity: they didn't have documented processes, shared training, or a common understanding of how the work was supposed to flow, and they were performing in a system that had never given them the tools to succeed.

What worked was 143 standard operating procedures, built in conference rooms alongside the operators who did the work. We walked through their day-to-day jobs, diagrammed the process flows, and captured the if-then-else decisions that only lived in people's heads.

Selected outcomes: attrition dropped from 50% to 1%, procedural errors fell 86%, critical system outages fell 80%, and all availability targets were met for the first time in the history of the organization. None of that came from diagnosing their performance first. It came from understanding the weight they were carrying.

Empathy is diagnostic discipline, applied to people.

What it looks like in practice:

  • Listen before diagnosing
  • Name the pressure before proposing a fix
  • Leave room for uncertainty until the answer is clear
  • Invite challenge, and then use the pushback

02

Give People the Context They Deserve

Your team deserves the same quality of context as your best AI prompts. Context determines decision quality.

There's a question I've started asking before every significant communication: Would I trust an AI to act on this information? If the context is incomplete, ambiguous, or missing the "why," the answer is no, and the same is true for the people on my team.

Context is the difference between a team that executes and a team that thinks. When people understand the constraints, the trade-offs and the business case behind a decision, they make better calls at every level: they escalate the right things, protect what matters, and don't burn energy on problems that were already solved upstream.

That principle extends beyond your direct team. In process excellence work every stakeholder has something urgent, the pressure to treat every request as the top priority is constant, and the temptation is to absorb it silently: accept everything, stay vague about timelines, and let people assume their work is moving. What that produces is a credibility problem that grows over time.

I learned a sharper version of this in my Scrum training and Professional Scrum Master certification in 2016. Scrum is built on a servant leadership foundation and lists transparency as one of its three pillars, and the discipline it brought into my practice is to make the queue visible: tell people what's being worked on now, what's waiting, and why the sequencing is what it is. When someone's request isn't at the top of the backlog, they deserve a direct answer: Your work is in queue. Here's what's ahead of it and why. Here's roughly when we expect to reach it. A clear answer, even a disappointing one, is more respectful than silence, because silence implies everything is in motion. Transparency is respect, and it gives people the information to make their own decisions.

Give your people what you give your best AI prompts: full context.

What it looks like in practice:

  • Explain the "why" before the "what"
  • Share the constraints behind each decision
  • Name what you don't know
  • Make the business case visible to the team
  • Make the backlog visible to stakeholders, and explain the prioritization logic
  • Deliver honest sequencing: what's first, what's waiting, and why, even when it disappoints

"His process maps provided clarity to roles and responsibilities through reorganizations allowing team members to understand how a process flows, where they fit in, and which steps need to be followed to eliminate duplication of efforts between teams."

03

Diagnose Before You Design

What appears to be the problem is rarely the actual problem. DMAIC separates signal from assumption.

One of the most expensive habits in organizational life is designing solutions before the diagnosis is complete. A process is slow, so we add headcount. A metric is red, so we add a meeting. A project is behind, so we add governance.

In most cases the intervention addresses the symptom and leaves the root cause intact, which means the problem comes back, usually worse, and now there's an extra meeting attached to it.

Lean Six Sigma trained me to hold the hypothesis loosely until the data is in. That means mapping the current state in detail (every handoff, every wait, every decision point) before proposing a future state, separating the presenting complaint from the constraint underneath it, and interviewing the operators as well as the managers.

One of the most consequential applications of this principle was the IT asset lifecycle. Application teams waited more than 17 weeks for compute infrastructure, and nobody had ever mapped the lifecycle end to end. I decomposed it into 400 activities across 64 systems and found the delay in the 110 handoffs between owners. We redesigned around those seams, removing 43% of the critical path steps and a third of the handoffs.

Selected outcomes: asset acquisition cycle time fell from over 17 weeks to under three (82%), and five years later the program had realized $61M against its $40M savings target (150%).

Solve the real problem, and the results follow.

What it looks like in practice:

  • Map current state before proposing future state
  • Separate symptoms from root causes
  • Interview operators before designing process
  • Hold the hypothesis loosely

04

Remove Friction

Automate and eliminate the drag that keeps talented people stuck in low-value tasks. Clear the path.

There is a category of organizational waste that rarely shows up in a budget: the time talented people spend doing work that a well-designed system should be doing for them.

I've seen a finance colleague spend three days on a manual comparison of spreadsheets, a task that existed only because no one had built the automation. I've seen automated dispatch stall because nobody governed the work area map underneath it, and operations teams build informal data systems in Excel because the enterprise system didn't give them what they needed.

Each of these is a friction problem, and in each one the constraint was the system the people worked in.

Removing friction also keeps people. The 50% to 1% attrition story is a friction story as much as an empathy story, because people leave systems that make it impossible to succeed.

When people have tools and clarity to do the work well, they stay.

What it looks like in practice:

  • Find the single constraint slowing the system
  • Automate the repeatable and elevate the rest
  • Simplify the handoff
  • Make the invisible visible with data

"Jeff's gift for critical thinking, process improvement and attention to detail helped us to automate a task that took 3 days of manual 'stare & compare' into a mouse click that compiled the data in less than 5 minutes."

Collin Ice, Verizon FP&A colleague

05

Invest in Others' Growth

Measure success by whether the people around you are stronger for having worked with you, as well as by the outcomes you delivered.

This is the principle that is hardest to sustain under pressure, because investing in someone's growth takes years to pay off and most operational environments are measured by the quarter.

I discovered early in my career what made me want to get up and go to work. It was the chance to leave a fingerprint on the business: a team, and the process they run, measurably more effective and efficient than when I joined them. Sharing the frameworks, pairing stretch assignments with coaching, and transferring knowledge freely are how that happens, and they outlast any single outcome. On the asset lifecycle program, I mentored eight Green Belts through certification on DMAIC projects of their own.

I try to measure this directly, with a leading indicator that shows up before the outcomes do: Is this person's capability growing? Do they have something now they didn't have three months ago? In my experience the answer to that question predicts sustained performance better than any metric I've tracked.

The team that worked with me should be measurably stronger for it.

What it looks like in practice:

  • Name what people do well, specifically
  • Pair stretch assignments with support
  • Share the framework along with the answer
  • Measure the team's growth as a personal metric

"When I had the pleasure of working with Jeff, I referred to him as 'the tide that lifts all ships' due to his support for teammates and investment in our organization's internal data and processes."

Jeff Parker, Strategy, Transformation & Innovation Leader, Verizon

06

Measure What Changes

Improvement claimed without a baseline is just a story. Rigorous measurement converts intent into evidence.

This is the principle underneath all the others, and the one that servant leadership frameworks most often leave out.

Empathy, transparency, removing friction and investing in growth are all human-centered practices, and measurement is what turns them into results. And in the organizations I've worked in (high accountability, data-driven, answerable to boards), intentions don't survive the next budget cycle.

The discipline I took from Lean Six Sigma is that no improvement is claimed without a defensible baseline, and the story of what changed is the measured delta from that baseline.

This discipline also protects the people doing the work. When outcomes are measured and attributed accurately, teams get credit for what they accomplished, and when improvements are claimed loosely, no one's contribution is ever truly seen. Five years after the asset lifecycle business case was approved, nobody had established whether it was ever delivered, so I went back to the same asset data, counted the decommissions in SQL, and found $61M realized against the $40M target. That kind of proof is what earns the next investment.

If you can't measure the delta, you can't own the result.

What it looks like in practice:

  • Establish the baseline before claiming progress
  • Build measurement infrastructure alongside the solution
  • Make KPIs visible to the people doing the work
  • Attribute outcomes with rigor and evidence

"…providing the critical reporting to build robust, data-backed business cases for both requesting additional headcount and justifying workforce reductions or reallocations during periods of changing demand."

Kristine Reelfs, Global Network Operations, Verizon

The Practice

These six principles came from three decades of working through operational complexity alongside people who were carrying weight that deserved to be taken seriously. They've held up in 24x7 operations centers and in SVP-level executive reviews, in workforce planning models and in conference rooms where I was building process documentation alongside technicians, and in Lean Six Sigma project charters and quiet conversations about what a team needs to succeed.

What I know from experience is that none of the six works in isolation. Empathy without measurement produces well-intentioned guesswork, and measurement without empathy produces a surveillance culture. Removing friction matters most when you understand the weight the person doing the work is carrying, and investing in growth goes further when the context is transparent.

I haven't lived these six things perfectly. There have been moments (under deadline pressure, in difficult stakeholder conversations, in seasons when I moved too fast) when I skipped the diagnosis, shortcut the context, or let measurement slide until after the fact. What I can say is that the moments I've struggled most have also been the moments these principles called me back. That's what a practice does. It gives you something to return to.

"Lead with empathy. Give your team and your stakeholders the context they deserve. Diagnose before you design. Remove friction. Invest in growth. Measure what changes. Do those six things consistently, and the results take care of themselves."

What's the hardest of these to sustain when the pressure is on? I'm curious what breaks down first, and what's helped you hold it.
