How to See the Forest
and the Trees

SIPOC, Adding Accountability

Jeffrey Benson  ·  2025

Forest & Trees Series  ·  Part I  |  Part II  |  Part III  |  Part IV  |  Part V  |  Part VI
Originally published on LinkedIn — View or comment there →

To see the forest and the trees, you develop the capability to step back and see the big picture, while simultaneously being able to zoom in and observe the trees. This series of articles will teach some practical things that will help you to cultivate this skill.

In Part I we introduced the Context Diagram and the four ICOM arrows that define the boundary of any process. In Part II we decomposed that context into an Activity Node Tree. In Part III we applied ICOMs to every node in that tree, tracing the flow from activity to activity.

The asset lifecycle from Part I ran across two SVP organizations, and each one owned part of it. Once the 110 handoffs were on the model, I framed them as divided ownership rather than poor team performance, which made a joint redesign possible. To get there, every handoff needed a named owner on each side.

The ICOMs tell us what flows through a process, what governs each activity, and what enables it to execute. They do not tell us who is on each end.

Who provides the inputs? Who receives the outputs? Until we name the people, teams, or organizations on both ends of the process, nobody is accountable for the diagram. SIPOC adds that accountability.

What is SIPOC?

SIPOC is a framework widely used in Lean Six Sigma. The acronym stands for:

  • S

    Supplier

    Who provides the inputs or performs the activity (an internal team, an external vendor, another process, an individual, or an automated system)

  • I

    Input

    What is provided (the materials, information, or resources that enter the process and are consumed or transformed)

  • P

    Process

    The activities that transform the inputs into outputs (typically shown at the high-level L1 view)

  • O

    Output

    What the process produces (the results, deliverables, or products that flow to the customer)

  • C

    Customer

    Who receives the outputs (internal or external, the next process in line or the end recipient)

The standard SIPOC template: five columns, with stakeholders named on both ends.

Notice what SIPOC adds to the ICOMs: a named Supplier on the left and a named Customer on the right.

Notice also what SIPOC does not include: Controls and Mechanisms. SIPOC has no element for the external standards or constraints that govern the process, and no element for the tools and equipment used to execute it.

Both frameworks describe structure and flow (something goes in, an activity transforms it, and something comes out), and both include whoever performs the activity. In ICOM, the performer is a Mechanism. In SIPOC, the performer is a Supplier, because a supplier provides the inputs or performs the activity. The performer can be a person like the Baker, or an automated system when the activity is fully automated. SIPOC also lists the Customers who receive the outputs, which ICOM does not. ICOM lists the Controls, and the tools and equipment used in the activity (the oven, the mixing bowl), which SIPOC does not.

Only SIPOC has

  • Suppliers of the inputs
  • Customers (who receives the outputs)

Only ICOM has

  • Controls (what governs the activity)
  • Tools and equipment used in the activity

Both have Inputs, Outputs, the activity between them, and whoever performs it, a person or an automated system (a Mechanism in ICOM, a Supplier in SIPOC). Used together, they describe an activity completely.

How SIPOC is Traditionally Taught

SIPOC grew out of the Total Quality Management programs of the late 1980s, which treated every step in a process as a customer of the step before it (the idea Kaoru Ishikawa called "next operation as customer"). In most Lean Six Sigma training today, SIPOC is introduced as a high-level process overview (a single diagram that captures the process on one page). The Process column shows the major steps at the L1 level, the same level we identified in Part II. The Supplier and Customer columns list the key stakeholders at the boundaries of the process.

It is useful, and it is fast to build. It gives leadership a quick stakeholder map that answers two questions: who feeds this process, and who depends on it? For a first conversation with a sponsor or an executive team, it is often the right level of detail.

But it is also incomplete, and we will come back to why at the end of this article.

Application: Baking Brownies - The Traditional L1 SIPOC

Let's apply the traditional SIPOC approach to the Baking Brownies process we have been building throughout this series.

The Baking Brownies SIPOC at L1 (the traditional single-page process overview).

Supplier

Who provides the inputs? The Fuel Provider supplies the fuel for the oven, and the Grocery Store Clerk supplies the ingredients. The Oven Operator and the Baker are suppliers too, because they perform the activities and provide their skill and effort to the process. Listing the suppliers makes it clear that this process depends on someone else delivering before it can begin.

Input

The inputs are the brownie mix, eggs, water, grease for the pan, fuel for the oven, and dish washing detergent (the same items we identified as Inputs with the ICOMs). SIPOC and ICOM agree on what enters the process. The difference is that SIPOC now tells us who provides them.

Process

The Process column lists the high-level activities at L1, the same four steps from Part II: A1 Preheat Oven, A2 Mix Ingredients, A3 Bake & Serve Brownies, and A4 Cleanup. In the traditional application, this column stays at L1 so that the SIPOC fits on one page. A traditional SIPOC shows us the forest. In Part V we will add the trees.

Output

The outputs are the baked brownies and, as we identified in Part I, also defective brownies, trash, and clean pans and utensils (the dirty pans go to A4 Cleanup inside the process). Good SIPOC practice, like good ICOM practice, lists the defect outputs too. You cannot find out where and why a process produces bad outputs until you admit that it does.

Customer

Who receives the outputs? The Baker's Family and Guests (the people the brownies are made for), and the Baker, who needs clean pans for the next batch. Listing the customer ties the process to its purpose. We preheat the oven, mix the ingredients, and bake and serve the brownies to deliver a specific result to specific people.

What SIPOC Adds, and What It Does Not

The traditional L1 SIPOC gives us something the ICOMs could not: named accountability at the boundaries of the process.

We know that inputs come from the Fuel Provider and the Grocery Store Clerk, and that the Oven Operator and the Baker perform the work. We know that outputs go to the Baker's Family and Guests. If there is a problem with the inputs (wrong ingredients, wrong quality), we know who to work with to resolve it. If the outputs do not meet expectations, we know whose requirements we are failing to satisfy.

But the traditional L1 SIPOC has a limitation. It treats the process as a single unit. It tells us who provides to the process and who receives from it, treating it as one unit. It says nothing about the accountability inside the process, between activities.

When A1 (Preheat Oven) produces its output, who is the customer of that output? It is A3 (Bake & Serve Brownies), not the Baker's Family. A3 depends on A1 delivering a preheated oven before it can begin. That is an internal supplier-customer relationship that the traditional L1 SIPOC cannot see.

That accountability between activities only becomes visible when you apply SIPOC at every activity node in the decomposition tree.

Most Lean Six Sigma training stops at L1, with one SIPOC per process. On the asset lifecycle I did not stop there. I ran SIPOC together with ICOMs at every node of the Activity Node Tree, and in 2013 that SIPOC and ICOM tree-node model was nominated and presented at Verizon's Lean Six Sigma Best Practice Conference. When I showed the method to a Master Black Belt coach, he told me that SIPOC at every node is how the tool was meant to be applied, and that training people to use it only as a high-level process map is an unfortunate reduction of scope for it.

In Summary

SIPOC adds what the ICOMs cannot: named suppliers (who provide the inputs or perform the activity) and named customers (who receive the outputs). ICOM adds what SIPOC cannot: the controls that govern each activity and the tools and equipment used in it. Together they describe each activity completely.

But as traditionally taught, SIPOC stops at the high level. It gives you the stakeholder map for the process (the forest), and none for the activities within it (the trees).

In Part V we will apply SIPOC at every node in the decomposition tree, the same way we applied ICOMs in Part III. When we combine the two frameworks at every level of decomposition, we can see for each activity who supplies it, what flows through it, what governs it, what enables it, and who depends on it.

We will also see how the entire model fits in one spreadsheet.
