Running It as a Control: Charts, Review Bands and Reaction Plans for an AI Judge

Jeffrey Benson  ·  2026

Measurement Series  ·  Part 1  |  Part 2  |  Part 3  |  Bridge  |  AI Evaluation 1  |  AI Evaluation 2  |  AI Evaluation 3

Since the end of September, the AI judge in my job search pipeline has passed every full qualification test it has run, re-tested each time its version changed. Over its ten most recent tests, its agreement with my "meets" calls has ranged from 86% to 93%, against a bar of 85%. I was pleased with that record until I did the arithmetic.

Each test checks the judge against the 28 to 31 postings in my answer key that I graded "meets". On a test that small, a judge that agrees with me 89% of the time over the long run will still come in below 85% about one time in five, by luck alone. A single pass does not prove the judge is good, and a single fail would not have proven it had gone bad.

That is the subject of this last article. The Mapping showed how to qualify an AI judge. What Changes When the Appraiser Is a Model showed why it needs more watching than a person does. Now we will run it as a control: charts on the judge, the band of uncertain cases that goes to a person, the reaction plan for the day something moves, and the events that call for a fresh study.

This article will not cover monitoring software or vendor dashboards. Rather, we will use the same tools from Part 3 (control charts, error-proofing and the control plan) and point them at the judge.

Chart the judge

One test is a snapshot

Let's put them on a p-chart, the chart from Part 3 for a yes-or-no result as a share of items.

1. The question it answers. Has the judge's agreement with people changed, or is this the ordinary up and down of a small test?

2. What goes in. Each test's count of agreements and the number of items checked, in time order.

3. What it does. The center line is the overall rate: 261 agreements out of 294 checks, or 88.8%. The limits come from how much a share of about 29 items can move by chance. For 29 items they run from about 71% to 100%.

4. How to read it. All ten tests sit well inside the limits, so the judge looks steady. Now notice where my pass bar of 85% falls: inside the limits, close to the middle. A single test at 83% would be ordinary noise for this judge. It would take a run of low tests to show a change. The bar is a reasonable target, but a single small test cannot tell whether the judge meets it.

For the formula-minded

Limits = 0.888 ± 3 × √(0.888 × 0.112 ÷ 29) = 0.888 ± 0.176, so about 0.71 to 1.06 (a limit above 100% is set to 100%). The chance that a judge with a true rate of 88.8% agrees on 24 or fewer of 29 items (below 85%) is about 22%, from the binomial distribution.

5. Where we use it. On any judge we test more than once. There are three ways to make the check sharper: test on more items (with 100 items the limits narrow to about 79% to 98%), judge the chart's pattern over several tests, and use the CUSUM or EWMA charts from Part 3, which catch a slow, steady slide in about a quarter of the time a basic chart takes (JMP).

What to watch

Four things to chart on a judge

Agreement with people is the most important thing to watch, and it is also the most expensive, because every point needs a person to check items. Some of the other signals need no people at all. This is the set I'd start with. It is my own synthesis, since I found no published standard for charting an AI judge in production:

What to chartNeeds people?How oftenWhat it catches
Agreement with people on a random audited sampleYesWeekly or monthly, or on every new versionThe judge drifting away from the people it stands in for
Share of each verdict (pass, fail, borderline)NoDailyA judge turning more lenient or harsher, or a change in what is coming in
Verdicts on a frozen set of itemsNoWeekly, and after any changeAny change at all, since the right answers never move
The model version that answeredNoEvery callA vendor update under the same name

Two cautions from Part 3 apply here too. At thousands of verdicts a day, a basic p-chart on the share of each verdict will alarm almost every day, and the Laney p′ chart adjusts its limits for that (Minitab). And a blend of very different items will signal on the mix alone, so chart each kind of item separately.

My pipeline does the last two rows today. It asks the vendor for a fixed model version, checks the version named in every answer, and leaves any answer from a different version out of its test results. It also keeps 10 frozen postings, and any changed verdict on them is a signal.

The review band

Which verdicts go to a person?

In Part 1 we learned that a marginal gage is used with care. For an AI judge, using it with care means sending the cases it is least sure of to a person. How many cases is that? The answer is a measurement decision first and a staffing decision second.

1. The question it answers. Which verdicts can the judge keep on its own, so that the errors among them stay below a rate we can live with?

2. What goes in. A calibration set: items with a person's answer, the judge's verdict and how sure the judge was of each one. Most judges can report a confidence, or a margin between two answers.

3. What it does. Sort the items from most sure to least sure. Starting at the top, keep adding items to the "judge decides" group while the error rate in that group stays under the target. Everything below the cutoff goes to a person.

Let's work one. A judge reviews 200 claims in a calibration set:

Judge's confidenceClaimsJudge wrongError rate if kept (running total)
0.90 and up12022 of 120 = 1.7%
0.80 to 0.904046 of 160 = 3.8%
0.70 to 0.8020511 of 180 = 6.1%
Below 0.7020819 of 200 = 9.5%

4. How to read it. If we can live with 3% errors on the verdicts the judge keeps, the cutoff is 0.90: the judge decides 60% of claims and people review 40%. If 5% is acceptable, the cutoff drops to 0.80: the judge decides 80% and people review 20%. The error target sets the cutoff, and the cutoff sets the workload. Halving the review band in this example costs about two more errors per hundred claims the judge keeps, and the cost of a wrong claim decision is what settles whether that trade is worth making.

5. Where we use it. Any process where an AI judge makes calls and people can review some of them.

Researchers at Thomson Reuters describe the same procedure for comparing answers: choose a target agreement level, trust the judge above the margin that meets it, and send the narrow-margin cases to human experts (Zhang et al. 2026). A more formal version calibrates the cutoff on a held-out set so that the rate of wrong verdicts among the accepted ones stays below a chosen level, with a statistical guarantee (Badshah, Emami and Sajjad).

My own pipeline has a rough version of this band. When every requirement behind a "fails" verdict is one the judge itself rates as a grey area, the posting is not pushed down my list. It goes to a section of my report called "Check the Required block", and I read it. I set that cutoff by judgment. The calibration above is how I'd check it.

Reaction plans

What we do when the chart moves

In Part 3 we said a reaction plan says who acts, how fast and what they do first. For an AI judge, these are the tiers I'd write. They are my design, not a published standard:

SignalFirst actionIf it is confirmed
An answer from a version we did not ask forSet those verdicts asideTreat it as a new judge: qualify it before it decides anything
A changed verdict on a frozen itemCheck for a vendor update, a change in our instructions, or a logging fault, and audit more items for a whileRe-qualify the judge on a fresh set of checked items
Share of a verdict outside its limitsCheck whether what is coming in has changed before blaming the judgeIf the judge changed, send the affected kind of item to people
Agreement with people below its lower limit, or a slow-drift signalSend the affected kind of item to people, and re-qualifyRoll back to the last version that passed, then recalibrate before trying the new one again

My pipeline has three rules of this kind, all learned the hard way. A new version of the judge has no authority until it passes its test: until then, its verdicts are recorded but cannot push a posting down my list. A test that was cut short (by a spending cap or an account error) is never scored, and if more than 10% of the answer key went unjudged, the result is recorded as "insufficient". And a change that touches only the facts the judge reads about me inherits the last passing result, with only the postings it had pushed down re-checked, while a change to the questions, the model or the inputs runs the full test again.

Re-study triggers

When to run the full study again

A chart watches for change between studies. Some events are a change on their own, and they call for the full qualification study from The Mapping before the judge decides anything again:

  • A new model version, announced or not. A study of two GPT-4 releases three months apart found accuracy on one task falling from 84% to 51% (Chen, Zaharia and Zou 2023).
  • A change to the instructions or the rubric, since the rubric is the judge's operational definition.
  • A change to what the judge is handed. When I changed how my pipeline splits postings into lines, 45 of the 133 postings in my answer key sent different lines to the judge, and the old test no longer described the new inputs.
  • A change to the answer key. My own re-grade in Part 2 found my calls repeat 83% of the time. Every correction I make to the key changes what the judge is scored against.
  • A chart signal that lasts after the first actions in the reaction plan.

The full arc

The same discipline, at a faster pace

Over seven articles we have followed one arc twice. For people making judgment calls: define the call, test the appraisers against an answer key, chart the process and the appraisers, error-proof the steps that fail, and hand a control plan to an owner. For an AI judge: the same steps, with a model in the appraiser's chair, an answer key made by people, and a fresh study every time the model, the instructions or the inputs change.

Every tool in this series is decades old. An AI judge asks us to use them more often, because the gage itself can change faster. A caliper drifts over months, and an examiner's habits shift over a year. An AI judge can change overnight, under the same name, and nobody may announce it.

Glossary

Terms used in this article

Pass bar
The agreement level a judge must reach on its qualification test.
p-chart
A control chart for a yes-or-no result as a share of items.
Audited sample
Items drawn at random from the judge's work and checked by a person.
Frozen set
Items whose right answers never change, re-judged to detect any change in the judge.
Calibration set
Items with a person's answer and the judge's confidence, used to choose a cutoff.
Review band
The verdicts the judge is least sure of, sent to a person.
Margin
How far apart the judge rates two answers; a narrow margin means an uncertain call.
Reaction plan
Who acts, how fast and what they do first when a chart signals.
Roll back
Return to the last model version that passed its test.
Re-study
Running the full qualification study again after a change.
