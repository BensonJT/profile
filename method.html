The method

Every process comes apart the same way.

There is no framework to buy here. There is a way of looking at work that has held up across eight domains and thirty-one years: take the process apart, instrument it, and use the measures to design what should replace it. It is simple enough to explain in about four minutes.

Part one · Decomposition

Start with one box. Keep breaking it down.

Take the whole thing as a single activity. Break it into the three to six activities that actually perform it. Break those down again. Keep going until each activity is small enough to assign to someone, measure, and improve. That last test is what tells you when to stop.

Why a brownie recipe. Because you can check it against your own kitchen. If the method holds on something trivially verifiable, the domain was never the hard part. I have run this on a bakery and on a two point two billion dollar spend portfolio, and the only thing that changed was how many levels deep it went.

Part two · What goes on every node

Four questions, asked at every level.

A decomposition tree on its own is just an outline. What turns it into a management instrument is answering the same four questions at every single activity.

Input
What is consumed or transformed here, and who supplies it? The supplier is the half most models leave out, and it is where accountability actually lives.
Control
What governs how this is done? Policy, standard, regulation, trigger. Controls constrain the work; they are not consumed by it. A recipe is a control. Eggs are an input. Most modelers lump both onto the left arrow, and the model stops being able to answer “what rule made this happen.”
Output
What leaves, and who receives it? Every output has to land somewhere downstream as an input or a control. When it does not, you have found either a dead end or a missing activity.
Mechanism
What tools and systems perform the work. People do not belong here. Human accountability lives in supplier and customer, so the mechanism arrow is reserved for the infrastructure that makes the work possible. That separation is what lets you ask “what would automation actually replace” and get a real answer.

Then two more, and this is the part that makes it diagnostic. At each activity: what is the measurement that says this is performing well or badly, and what is broken or at risk here right now? Those two fields turn a picture of how things work into an active account of where the friction is, and they are what make a future state model comparable to the current one rather than merely different from it.

Part three · How the work runs

Five steps, and the first one is the one everybody skips.

The output is sometimes a redesigned process, sometimes a data system, and usually both. The sequence does not change either way.

01

Listen, map, and align.

Conversations with everyone who touches the process, not just leadership looking at KPIs. Where does it hurt? What are you carrying that you should not have to carry? What workarounds has the team built because the official system does not work? At the same time the decomposition gets underway, mapping what actually happens rather than what the three-year-old presentation says happens. This is where most engagements rush, and it is why most engagements fail.

02

Fix the foundation before building on it.

You cannot model complexity on a broken base. If the underlying data is fragmented, analytics built on top amplify the problem rather than reveal it. If accountability is fragmented, a redesigned process inherits the same ambiguity. On one engagement this phase took three months and 41,326 ZIP codes before a single line of the capacity model was written. It is scope-dependent and it is non-negotiable: every solution built on a fragile foundation inherits that fragility.

03

Model what is. Then diagnose it.

The current state model captures what actually happens today, including the pain points, the defect rates, and the places where nothing is being measured at all. That last category is usually the most revealing.

Then the model gets read as an instrument rather than a picture. Where is the cycle time actually going? Which activity has no declared owner? Where does yield compound badly enough that the end-to-end number stops resembling any individual step? Diagnosis comes from the measures, not from the workshop. A room full of experienced people will confidently name the wrong bottleneck; the instrumented model will not.

04

Design what should be. The delta is the roadmap.

The future state is a second model, not a slide. Redesign the activities that matter most. Reassign who supplies the input and who receives the output, because most waste lives in the handoff rather than inside the box. Introduce the systems that remove the manual steps. Set target values for the measures that were failing.

Then compare the two. The difference between current and future state is the transformation roadmap, and because both sides are quantified, it arrives already costed. Not a list of recommendations to be argued over, a paired before and after where the business case is arithmetic.

Both models are a deliverable. The client keeps them, and they remain useful long after the engagement, because the next change gets measured against a baseline that already exists.

05

Build it, adapt it, hand it off.

Making the future state real is process work, governance work, and usually software: the procedures that remove the ambiguity, the ownership model that makes the handoffs someone's job, and the pipelines and reporting that let the team see whether any of it is working.

The build is iterative on purpose. A first version goes back to the team, and something useful happens: because they can finally see the process and the numbers together, they start asking questions they could not have articulated before. Is this step actually parallel? Can we flag when capacity drops below the threshold? Some of those are a small change, some send you back to the model. Either way the loop continues until the thing answers the questions that actually matter, not the ones we guessed at in week one.

Then it has to outlive me. Documentation, architecture, data flow, a maintenance playbook, and structured training, sized to what the organization can actually carry. A model nobody owns is a model that dies the quarter after I leave.

What guides it

Three foundations.

Diagnose with empathy

Find the true root cause, not the most visible symptom. Listen to the people carrying the weight before building anything. Behind most broken processes is someone carrying weight they should not have to carry, and the weight is almost always structural.

Automate to elevate

Free teams from manual work so they can do what only people can do. Infrastructure that runs, not analyses that sit in a drawer. And never automate a process that is not understood first, because automating a mess only makes the friction faster.

Model for reality

Surface the capacity, cost, and friction that have become too complex to see. Clean foundations before complex models. No improvement is real without a defensible baseline, and the delta is what proves the people doing the work earned the result.
