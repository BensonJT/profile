Approach

How I take a problem apart.

One method has held up across eight domains and 30+ years: break the process or problem down until each piece can be defined and measured, use the model’s insights to redesign it, implement it with the team, and hand it off to people who can run it. Below are the method, the five steps, and the six leadership principles behind them.

Part one · Decomposition

Start with one box. Keep breaking it down.

Take the whole thing as a single activity. Break it into the 3 to 6 activities that perform it. Break those down again. Keep going until each activity is small enough to assign to someone, measure, and improve. That last test is what tells you when to stop.

Why a brownie recipe. Because you can check it against your own kitchen. If the method holds on something trivially verifiable, the domain was never the hard part. I have run this on a bakery and on a $2.2B spend portfolio, and the only thing that changed was how many levels deep it went.

Part two · What goes on every node

Four questions, asked at every level.

A decomposition tree on its own is just an outline. What turns it into a management instrument is answering the same four questions at every single activity.

Input
What is consumed or transformed here, and who supplies it? The supplier is the half most models leave out, and it is where accountability lives.
Control
What governs how this is done? Policy, standard, regulation, trigger. Controls constrain the work, but the work does not consume them. A recipe is a control. Eggs are an input. Most modelers lump both onto the left arrow, and the model stops being able to answer “what rule made this happen.”
Output
What leaves, and who receives it? Every output has to land somewhere downstream as an input or a control. When it does not, you have found either a dead end or a missing activity.
Mechanism
What tools and systems perform the work. People do not belong here. Human accountability lives in supplier and customer, so the mechanism arrow is reserved for the infrastructure that makes the work possible. That separation is what lets you ask “what would automation actually replace” and get a real answer.

Then two more, and this is the part that makes it diagnostic. At each activity: what is the measurement that says this is performing well or badly, and what is broken or at risk here right now? Those two fields turn a picture of how things work into an active account of where the friction is, and they are what make a future state model comparable to the current one rather than merely different from it.

Part three · How the work runs

Five steps from problem to handoff.

The output is usually a redesigned process, a data system, or both. The sequence is the same either way.

01

Listen, map and align.

  • Talk with everyone who touches the process, not just the leaders reading the KPIs.
  • Ask where it hurts, what is done by hand, and what workarounds the team has built.
  • Start the decomposition: map what actually happens, not what the old slide says happens.

Rushing this step is the most common reason improvement efforts fail.

02

Fix the foundation first.

  • Fragmented data makes analytics amplify the problem instead of revealing it.
  • Fragmented ownership carries straight into any redesigned process.
  • Example: 3 months and 41,326 ZIP codes before the first line of a capacity model was written.

Everything built on a fragile foundation inherits the fragility.

03

Model the current state, then diagnose it.

  • Capture what happens today: pain points, defect rates, and what is not measured at all.
  • Use the model as an instrument: where the cycle time goes, which step has no owner, where yield compounds.

Diagnosis comes from the measures, not the workshop. A room of experts will often name the wrong bottleneck.

04

Design the future state. The difference is the roadmap.

  • Build the future state as a second model, not a slide.
  • Redesign the key activities and handoffs, where most waste lives, and remove manual steps.
  • Both models stay with the team as the baseline for the next change.

Because both models are measured, the roadmap arrives already costed. The business case is arithmetic.

05

Build, iterate and hand off.

  • Deliver the procedures, the ownership model, and the pipelines and reporting that show whether it works.
  • Iterate with the team: once they see the process and the numbers together, they ask better questions.
  • Hand off with documentation, a maintenance playbook, and an AI assistant trained on all of it.

A model nobody owns dies the quarter after I leave.

Leadership

Six leadership principles, each backed by a colleague.

Each one is a behavior, and the colleague quoted under it put it on the record independently.

Lead with empathy

Understand what someone is dealing with before judging their performance

01

Listen before diagnosing. Name the pressure before proposing a fix. Leave room for uncertainty instead of forcing an early answer. Invite challenge and then use the pushback. Empathy is diagnostic discipline, applied to people.

“Jeff is approachable, he listens to the team members, and pivots when necessary to implement their recommendations.”

Carolyn DeReus EAM program team member, Verizon

Give people the context you would give your best prompt

Context determines decision quality

02

Explain the why before the what. Share constraints, not just decisions. Name what you do not know. Make the business case visible to the team and the backlog visible to stakeholders, including the prioritization logic. Deliver honest sequencing even when it disappoints.

Every handoff includes full documentation and an AI assistant trained on it, so the team can ask how the process works and train the next person.

“His process maps provided clarity to roles and responsibilities through reorganizations allowing team members to understand how a process flows, where they fit in, and which steps need to be followed to eliminate duplication of efforts between teams.”

John V. Demetrio Director, Verizon IT Network Implementation

Diagnose before you design

What appears to be the problem is rarely the actual problem

03

Map current state before proposing future state. Separate symptoms from root causes. Interview the operators before designing the process. Hold the hypothesis loosely. Solving the real problem produces what the apparent fix never would.

“Jeffrey has always been and remains the person to turn to when there is a problem or effort so entangled that most do not know where to start…”

Sally Curran Verizon

Remove friction

Automate the drag that keeps talented people from their real work

04

When people have the tools and the clarity to do the work well, they stay. Most attrition that gets diagnosed as a culture problem is an ambiguity problem wearing a disguise.

“Jeff’s gift for critical thinking, process improvement and attention to detail helped us to automate a task that took 3 days of manual ‘stare & compare’ into a mouse click that compiled the data in less than 5 minutes.”

Collin Ice Verizon FP&A colleague

Invest in growth

Measure success by whether the people around you got stronger

05

The team that works alongside you should be measurably stronger for it. Build capability, not dependency. 8 Green Belts through full certification, a 4-person analytics team brought onto AI-assisted development, and 143 procedures written with the technicians rather than for them.

“When I had the pleasure of working with Jeff, I referred to him as ‘the tide that lifts all ships’ due to his support for teammates and investment in our organization’s internal data and processes.”

Jeff Parker Strategy, Transformation & Innovation Leader at Verizon; managed Jeffrey directly

Measure what changes

Improvement claimed without a baseline is just a story

06

No baseline, no claim. The delta is the proof, and it gives the people who did the work the credit they earned. That applies to my own work too: I measured one of my own code changes 3.3× slower than what it replaced, reverted it, and fixed the real constraint.

“Crucially, this model also became the backbone for our headcount modeling and reporting — providing the critical reporting to build robust, data-backed business cases for both requesting additional headcount and justifying workforce reductions or reallocations during periods of changing demand.”

Kristine Reelfs Global Network Operations, Verizon; her team used the force-to-load model daily

“Lead with empathy. Give your team and your stakeholders the context they deserve. Diagnose before you design. Remove friction. Invest in growth. Measure what changes. Do those six things consistently, and the results take care of themselves.”
