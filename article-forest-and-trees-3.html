How to See the Forest
and the Trees

ICOMs at Every Node

Jeffrey Benson  ·  2025

Forest & Trees Series  ·  Part I  |  Part II  |  Part III  |  Part IV  |  Part V  |  Part VI
Originally published on LinkedIn — View or comment there →

To see the forest and the trees, you develop the capability to step back and see the big picture, while simultaneously being able to zoom in and observe the trees. This series of articles will teach some practical things that will help you to cultivate this skill.

On the asset lifecycle model I described in Part I, the Activity Node Tree gave us 400 activities. When I added ICOMs at every node (together with SIPOC, which Part IV covers), the constraints and the 110 handoffs surfaced at the activity that owned each one.

In Part I we introduced the Context Diagram (the highest view of any process, labeled Level 0 or L0) and the ICOMs (Inputs, Controls, Outputs, and Mechanisms) that define its boundary. In Part II we learned how to decompose that context into an Activity Node Tree, breaking the process into three to six activities at each level until you have the depth your purpose requires.

In Part III we will apply the ICOMs to every activity node in the tree. When we do, the model begins to show how the activities depend on each other and where a process breaks down.

Every Activity Is a Process

In Part I, the ICOMs defined the boundary of the entire process at the context level (L0). An Activity Node Tree is a hierarchy of smaller processes nested inside a larger one. Every box in that tree (A1, A2, A2.3, A2.3.1) is itself a process. It has something flowing into it, something governing it, something it produces, and something enabling it to execute.

That means we can draw ICOMs for every activity, just as we did for the Context Diagram.

Every activity node in the decomposition tree has the same structure as the Context Diagram (its own Inputs, Controls, Outputs, and Mechanisms).

When you apply ICOMs at every node, three things happen:

  • You see the inputs and outputs of each activity: the specific materials, information, or decisions each activity requires and produces, in addition to those of the entire process.
  • You can trace the flow through the process, because the output of one activity almost always becomes an input, control, or mechanism for another. Following that flow tells you how the process works and where it does not.
  • You find what is missing: an output that nobody receives, an activity with no mechanism assigned to it, or a team working without any controls.

The Flow Between Activities

When we apply ICOMs across the Activity Node Tree, we can see how the activities connect to each other.

The output of one activity usually goes to another activity downstream, where it becomes one of three things:

An Input - it is consumed or transformed in the next step.

A Control - it becomes a standard or condition that governs how later work is executed.

A Mechanism - it becomes a tool or enabling resource that makes later work possible.

If you can trace each of these connections, the process is well understood. If an output is produced but no activity receives it, you have found a broken handoff. Broken handoffs are where work waits, and on the asset lifecycle that is where I found the delay.

Application: Baking Brownies - ICOMs at Every L1 Node

Let's return to the Baking Brownie process. In Part II we identified four high-level activities at L1:

A1Preheat Oven
A2Mix Ingredients
A3Bake & Serve Brownies
A4Cleanup

Now let's apply ICOMs to each activity individually, moving from the forest view down to the level of each tree.

A1 - Preheat Oven

  • InputFuel (electricity or gas) consumed by the oven to reach temperature
  • ControlsRecipe (specifies required temperature), Oven Performance (does this oven run hot or cool?), Safety Regulations
  • OutputPreheated Oven (the oven has reached the correct baking temperature)
  • MechanismsOven (the machine executing the activity), Oven Operator (who initiates, monitors, and verifies)

Notice how we name the output: "Preheated Oven." It states what this activity must deliver before the next step can begin. Also notice that Oven Performance is a control. Experienced bakers know whether their oven runs hot or cool and adjust for it. Writing it down as a control means the next baker can follow it too.

A2 - Mix Ingredients

  • InputsBrownie Mix, Eggs, Water, Grease (all consumed or transformed by this activity)
  • ControlsRecipe (specifies proportions and mixing method), Customer Preference (nut allergies, add-ins, consistency preferences)
  • OutputsBrownie Batter (primary), Trash (egg shells and packaging), Greased Pan (the pan is greased here and flows to A3)
  • MechanismsMixing Bowl & Spoon, Pan, Baker

The inputs to this activity (Brownie Mix, Eggs, Water, and Grease) are consumed and transformed. When A2 is complete, the ingredients no longer exist in their original form. They have become Brownie Batter. Note also the three outputs. The primary output (Brownie Batter) flows forward. The secondary output (Trash) exits the process. And the Greased Pan flows directly to A3 where it will be needed. The pan itself is a Mechanism, but greasing it changes it, so the Greased Pan leaves A2 as an output.

A3 - Bake & Serve Brownies

  • InputsBrownie Batter (from A2), Greased Pan (from A2)
  • ControlsRecipe (baking time and temperature), Customer Preferences (final product requirements), Oven Performance
  • OutputsBaked Brownies (primary), Defective Brownies (a burned batch), Dirty Pans & Utensils (flow to A4)
  • MechanismsPreheated Oven (ready from A1), Timer, Oven Mitt, Cooling Rack, Baker, Oven Operator

Look carefully at A3's inputs and mechanisms. Both of its inputs came from A2 (the Brownie Batter and the Greased Pan), and its primary mechanism, the Preheated Oven, was produced by A1. A3 cannot execute until A1 and A2 have done their work correctly, and the ICOMs put those dependencies on the diagram where everyone can see them.

A4 - Cleanup

  • InputsDirty Pans & Utensils (from A3), Dish Washing Detergent, Water
  • ControlsNone listed
  • OutputClean Pans & Utensils (ready for the next batch)
  • MechanismsTowels, Wash Cloths, Sink, Baker, Oven Operator

A4 is the kind of activity that is easy to leave off a model. Notice that our model has no control listed for it. What governs cleanup? Is there a standard for how clean is clean enough? We will come back to that question below.

Seeing the Flow

Now let's step back and look at all four activities together. The connected diagram below shows the full L1 process, with the cross-activity flows that link them.

The L1 Baking Brownies process with ICOMs at every node and the flows between activities.

The three cross-activity flows

→

A1 → A3: The Preheated Oven is the output of Preheat Oven and a mechanism for Bake & Serve Brownies. Like every mechanism, the oven is not consumed by the baking. A3 cannot start until A1 has delivered this output.

→

A2 → A3: Brownie Batter and Greased Pan are outputs of Mix Ingredients and inputs to Bake & Serve Brownies. They are consumed and transformed by the baking. Without them, A3 has nothing to bake.

→

A3 → A4: Dirty Pans & Utensils are an output of Bake & Serve Brownies and an input to Cleanup. A4 turns them back into Clean Pans & Utensils for the next batch.

Preheat Oven and Mix Ingredients must both succeed before Bake & Serve Brownies can begin. If either one fails to produce its output, Bake & Serve Brownies cannot run.

Beyond Brownies

Why does this matter beyond brownies? In business processes, activities are rarely this clearly connected. Teams receive work from upstream without knowing what form it should arrive in. They produce outputs without knowing who needs them or in what format. They run activities without documented controls, relying on the expertise of a few people instead of a standard. And sometimes an activity appears in the process documents with no mechanism assigned to it at all (the step was identified, but nobody owns it).

When you apply ICOMs to every activity node, these problems become visible. Ask these four questions at every node:

Inputs

Can we name what this activity receives? If not, the handoff from upstream is undefined, and the activity is running on assumptions.

Controls

What governs this activity? If the answer is "whoever is doing it decides," there is no standard, and no consistency when that person is unavailable. (That is the question our own A4 Cleanup raised.)

Outputs

Can we name what this activity produces, and who receives it next? If not, work may be produced that the next step never picks up.

Mechanisms

What enables this activity to execute (tools, systems, or people)? If the answer is unclear, the activity cannot be reliably staffed or resourced.

Defining the ICOMs at every node takes the process out of a few people's heads and puts it where you can improve it and train someone new on it.

In Summary

Applying ICOMs at every activity node turns the Activity Node Tree into a model you can use to diagnose a process. For each activity you now know what it needs, what governs it, what it produces, and what enables it. By tracing the flows between activities, you can see the connections (and the broken handoffs) that decide whether the process works.

In Part II we learned to build the Activity Node Tree. In Part III we added ICOMs to every node. Now we can look at any one activity (a tree) and see how it connects to the activities around it and to the rest of the process (the forest).

But there is one more thing the ICOMs cannot show us: accountability. ICOMs tell you what flows through the process, what governs it, and what enables it. They do not tell you who provides the inputs or who receives the outputs. In Part IV we will add that accountability by identifying the suppliers and the customers of each activity.
