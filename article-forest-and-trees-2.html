How to See the Forest
and the Trees

Activity Node Analysis

Jeffrey Benson  ·  2019

Forest & Trees Series  ·  Part I  |  Part II  |  Part III  |  Part IV  |  Part V  |  Part VI
Originally published on LinkedIn — View or comment there →

To see the forest and the trees, you develop the capability to step back and see the big picture, while simultaneously being able to zoom in and observe the trees. This series of articles will teach some practical things that will help you to cultivate this skill.

In Part I we introduced the Context Diagram (we refer to this as the Level 0 diagram - the highest view of the process). The context diagram is the broadest view of the forest. In Part II we will learn how to decompose the forest to begin to see the individual trees and understand how they fit into the forest. In IDEF0 terms, this is called building an Activity Node Tree.

We have the Context Diagram (L0). What's next?

In IDEF0 we use Activity Node Analysis to break down the Context Diagram's process (Level 0) into the activities that make up that process. We systematically decompose each activity until we get the level of understanding required by the process model's purpose and viewpoint.

The 3-6 Rule: When decomposing the process into the activities that make up the process, each level should be articulated in three to no more than six activities. If you see more than six activities, then roll them up at a higher level of decomposition and articulate those additional details at a lower level.

It is recommended to take each process down two levels of decomposition (decompose L0 into L1 and L2). Using the paradigm of L0 being the highest level of decomposition, L1 would be the high level process, and L2 would be a lower level articulation of the process. But sometimes it is necessary to decompose an activity more deeply.

For example, if the lower level of decomposition (L2) requires more than six activities to articulate it, you might summarize one activity and decompose that particular activity to a lower level of detail (L3), as shown in this Activity Node Tree graphic:

The Activity Node Tree: L0 decomposes to L1 and L2, and to L3 only where needed.

Once you get beyond the lower level of process decomposition, known as L2, it is not necessary to take all activities to a deeper level of articulation. As shown above, we might only decompose Activity 2.3 more deeply, seeing Activities A2.3.1, A2.3.2, and A2.3.3 at L3.

Note that the Activity numbering scheme always references the context:

A1, A2, A3 High level activities at L1, direct children of the Context Diagram A1.1, A1.2, A1.3 L2 decomposition of Activity 1 A2.1, A2.2, A2.3 L2 decomposition of Activity 2 A2.3.1, A2.3.2, A2.3.3 L3 decomposition of Activity 2.3, only needed when that activity is complex enough to warrant it

Remember, it is only necessary to decompose to the level required by your purpose and viewpoint. As a general rule, articulating the process as a context diagram (L0), high level process (L1), and the lower level (L2) is normally sufficient and a good best practice, but there may be situations where decomposing a process well beyond L2 may be required.

When might we go deeper into the forest and need to decompose a process beyond L2?

We may have to go much deeper if we are modeling an end to end or full lifecycle process. For example, if you are an organizational executive or a business transformation office, and you want to understand the critical activities associated with how your organization develops and delivers your goods and services across several lines of business, starting with the first three levels would be a great first step.

Then each of the line of business executives below their executive leader may want to take their activities down to three levels of decomposition, and the functional leaders that report to them may wish to do the same. Following that approach, it would be possible to have a process articulated through 6 or more levels of decomposition with hundreds of activities identified that all roll up to the 6-8 activities that make up the high level process (L1).

The asset lifecycle model I described in Part I worked this way. It started from one A0, broke into 8 activities at L1 and about 6 under each at L2, reached L3 everywhere, and went to L4, L5 or L6 only where the depth was needed (400 activities in all). That is what it means to see the forest and the trees. The context diagram (L0) is the forest and the activities at the lowest level of decomposition (Ln) are the trees. Because every activity number traces back up through its parent activities to A0, you can work at any level without losing sight of the forest.

Application: Baking Brownie Process

I am sure you are following along, but to help crystallize the concepts, let's apply this to the Baking Brownie process. Remember in Part I we built a Level 0 Context Diagram for the Baking Brownies process:

The L0 Context Diagram from Part I, our starting point for decomposition.

We are documenting this process from the viewpoint of the Baker. Let's take the L0 Context Diagram for the Baking Brownies process and decompose it to the high level activities that make up that process (the L1 level of detail).

When identifying activities, it is a best practice to use the formula verb noun and to keep the language as simple as possible.

In this process we might say the high level steps are:

A1 Preheat Oven
A2 Mix Ingredients
A3 Bake Brownies

L0 → L1: the three high level activities of the Baking Brownies process.

Next let's break each of these process steps into the next level of detail. What might the next level of decomposition (L2) be for the Baking Brownie process?

A1 Preheat Oven
  • A1.1Check Recipe
  • A1.2Determine Temp (adjust for oven performance)
  • A1.3Ignite Oven
A2 Mix Ingredients
  • A2.1Add Brownie Mix
  • A2.2Add Water
  • A2.3Add Eggs
  • A2.4Mix
  • A2.5Pour
A3 Bake Brownies
  • A3.1Place in Oven
  • A3.2Set Timer
  • A3.3Remove
  • A3.4Cool

In Summary

Once you have identified the context (L0) and decomposed that process into two or more levels of decomposition (L1, L2, …), you are now beginning to see both the forest and the trees.

But there is more we can do to fully comprehend the interrelationships between the forest and the trees.

Do you remember the ICOMs (Inputs, Controls, Outputs, and Mechanisms) discussed in Part I? In Part III of this article series we will start to understand the role of each activity within the process by identifying the critical inputs, outputs, controls, and mechanisms for each activity at each level of decomposition. IDEF0 provides a really helpful modeling diagram to illustrate these relationships, and we will introduce this in Part III.
