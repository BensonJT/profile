The Mapping: Measurement System Analysis for an AI Judge

Jeffrey Benson  ·  2026

Measurement Series  ·  Part 1  |  Part 2  |  Part 3  |  Part 4  |  Part 5  |  Part 6  |  Part 7

In late September I approved a plan for a new AI judge in my job search pipeline. The judge's job is to read a posting and decide whether I meet its Required qualifications, the same call I make myself on my answer key. The first line of the plan says the judge is to be treated as a new appraiser, and that it gets the full measurement-system study: agreement with my answer key, repeatability, calibration, and stability across model versions. Nothing it said would move a posting in my rankings until it passed.

The AI judge I had tried before it had not passed. By late September it had failed the same test eight rounds in a row. On September 24 it agreed with my "meets" calls 77% of the time against a bar of 85%, and it caught 56% of the postings I fail against a bar of 70%.

Reading the plan back now, I see a Measure phase with the word "model" where the word "examiner" would go. That is the idea behind the second half of this series. Everything we learned about measuring people who make judgment calls applies, term by term, to an AI that makes the same calls.

This article will not explain how language models work, nor survey the AI evaluation tools on the market. Rather, we will map each idea from the first half of this series onto an AI judge, look at the stacked gage underneath every AI evaluation, and see why the 10% and 30% rule from the plant floor does not transfer.

The mapping

The judge is an appraiser

First, what do we mean by an AI judge? It is a language model (the kind of AI behind tools like ChatGPT and Claude) given an item and a question, and asked for a call. Does this customer reply follow our refund policy? Does this ticket belong in the billing queue? Does this candidate meet the posting's Required qualifications? The call comes back as a category, the same way an examiner's does.

Here is the mapping, term by term, with a claims desk beside it and my own pipeline as the worked case:

The first row matters most. The gage is never the model alone. Change the instructions, the background text or the model version and we have a new gage, and the study we ran on the old one no longer describes it. My pipeline puts a version label on every AI review that changes whenever any of those pieces change. We met the same rule for software appraisers in the Bridge article.

Repeatability

Ask it again

A rules engine gives the same answer to the same input every time. A language model may not. It builds each answer one word at a time, with a little built-in randomness in which word comes next, and the setting that turns that randomness down (called temperature) reduces it without always removing it. The plant-floor question comes back: if we ask the same judge about the same item twice, do we get the same call?

1. The question it answers. Does the judge agree with itself?

2. What goes in. The answer-key items, sent to the judge two or three times with nothing changed, and every call it made.

3. What it does. Count the items whose call changed between passes, and look at how far each one moved.

4. How to read it. The share that changed is the flip rate. My pipeline's bar for the judge is 2% or less. In its test, I sent all 115 answer-key postings at the time through the judge three times. Its Required call changed on 5 of the 115 postings, and every change was a single step: meets to partial, or partial to fails, never meets to fails. Counting every individual answer the judge gives along the way, the flip rate was 1.6%, inside the bar.

5. Where we use it. Before trusting any AI judge, and again after any change to it.

Two more things I learned from my own judges. The earlier judge, run with its randomness turned down to zero, gave identical answers on repeat. The newer one did not: in a three-call test on the vendor's own sample, its confidence numbers moved by about 0.03 each time. And in late October, when I added a new fact to the fact sheet the judge reads about me (a fact that had nothing to do with the postings in question), 6 of 209 postings changed their verdict, in both directions. That is what noise looks like on a judge, and it is why I'd never judge a model on a single pass.

There is good news here too. In Part 1 we worried about the examiner who remembers the claim. A model keeps no memory from one call to the next, so it is the one appraiser we can re-test truly blind, as often as we like.

The stacked gage

The answer key is a gage too

On a plant floor, the master part is machined to a known size. In AI evaluation, the answer key came from people. That means there are two measurement systems stacked on top of each other: the people who made the key, and then the judge we score against it. If we skip the first, we are scoring the judge against a master nobody calibrated.

Let's see what that does to a score. Suppose my answer key has 100 postings, and on a careful re-read I would change my call on 10 of them. (My re-grade in Part 1 is the reason I think 10 is not an unfair guess: on 17 hard postings I changed 8.) Now score two judges against my key:

  • Judge A is right on all 100. It disagrees with my key on the 10 postings where I was wrong. Its score: 90 of 100.
  • Judge B is wrong on 10. Five of its mistakes are the same five mistakes I made, so on those it matches my key. The other five are on postings I got right, so on those it disagrees with my key. It also disagrees with my key on the other 5 postings where I was wrong, because there it is right. Its score: 100 − 5 − 5 = 90 of 100.

A perfect judge and a judge that copies half my mistakes get the same score. The key cannot tell them apart. This is the stacked-gage problem, and it is why the first measurement in any AI evaluation should be people against people.

Here is that first measurement in five steps.

1. The question it answers. Is our answer key consistent enough to grade a judge with?

2. What goes in. A sample of the items, judged blind by two people (or by one person twice, weeks apart, as in my re-grade study for Part 2), plus the AI judge's call on the same items.

3. What it does. Compute the agreement between the two people first. Then compute the judge's agreement with each person.

4. How to read it. The people's agreement is the baseline. If two careful people agree on 90% of the items, a judge that agrees with each of them about 90% of the time is judging the way a person does. MLflow's guide to calibrating AI judges puts it directly: measure human-human agreement first, because "a judge cannot be more aligned than its labelers" (MLflow). NVIDIA researchers built a benchmark on this comparison, scoring each model's agreement with each person against a person-to-person baseline (kappa 0.801 on their data) (Han et al. 2025).

5. Where we use it. Before any AI judge is scored, and every time the answer key grows.

Is that baseline a hard ceiling? Not quite. A simulation study found a model can score above the agreement between the people who labeled the data, as long as the model is reasonably well specified (Richie, Grover and Tsui 2022). So I'd treat a judge score near or above the human baseline as a reason to look closely at the disagreements, item by item, before celebrating or dismissing it.

Two more habits from my own key. I count the items the people could not agree on, and I report the judge's score with and without them. Leaving them out is fine as long as the count is reported, because a long list of contested items is evidence that the definition needs work. And when the people disagree a lot, the fix is the definition first, the same lesson as Part 1. MLflow's guide makes the same point about rubrics: if your two best reviewers agree only 70% of the time, no judge instructions will fix it.

Acceptance

Why the 10% and 30% rule does not transfer

In Part 1 we met the plant-floor rule: if the measuring accounts for under 10% of the variation, the gage is acceptable, 10% to 30% is marginal, and over 30% means fix the gage first (Minitab). Can we hold an AI judge to that rule?

Usually not. The rule splits variation in readings taken on a scale (a length, a weight, a time). Most AI judges return a category: meets or fails, billing or technical, follows policy or not. There is no variation on a scale to split. In my mapping, the rule applies only when a judge gives a score on a scale, say 1 to 10, and then the matching study asks what share of the variation in scores the judge itself causes. For category calls, we are back to the tools from Part 1:

  • Agreement with the answer key, corrected for luck (kappa), and Gwet's AC1 beside it when one answer dominates. My answer key is 79% "fails", so I report both.
  • A bar set by the cost of a wrong call. Practitioner bars for AI judges tend to borrow the same labels we met in Part 1. MLflow's guide, for one, suggests reworking the rubric when kappa falls below about 0.61, the bottom of the "substantial" band.
  • The human baseline from the section above, which is the better-grounded comparison of the two.

My own pipeline uses two bars, because the two kinds of error cost me different things (the miss and the false alarm from the Bridge article). The judge's verdict can only push a posting down my list. The error I most want to avoid is a wrong "fails" on a posting I meet, which could hide a job I should apply for. The agree bar (of the postings I say I meet, how many the judge also says I meet) measures that, and it is the stricter one at 85%. The catch bar (of the postings I say I fail that an earlier AI check had passed, how many this judge flags) is 70%. The current judge passed both on its most recent full test (November 2), at 90% and 87%.

The rest of the list

Bias, linearity and stability

  • Bias is a judge that leans one way: more lenient or harsher than the answer key. Reporting the two error rates apart, as my two bars do, is how it shows up. A single overall score would hide it.
  • Linearity is whether that lean stays the same across the range. A judge can be reliable on the clear fits and the clear misfits and shaky in the middle, which is where the decisions are hard. Every one of my judge's repeat-run changes involved the middle answer, "partial".
  • Stability is drift over time, and for AI it is a bigger worry than for people. A hosted model can change under the same name. A 2023 study compared two releases of GPT-4 three months apart and found its accuracy at telling prime numbers from composite ones fell from 84% to 51% (Chen, Zaharia and Zou 2023). My pipeline asks for a fixed model version, checks that the version that answered is the one it asked for, and keeps 10 frozen postings the judge can be asked to re-judge at any time.

Is there evidence that a strong AI judge can match people? Yes, on some tasks. The paper that launched much of this field found that strong judges like GPT-4 matched human preferences over 80% of the time, the same level at which humans agreed with each other (Zheng et al. 2023). That was measured on their tasks, against their people. The study we just mapped is how we find out whether it is true on ours.

Your turn

Who wrote your answer key?

If your team uses an AI judge, or is about to, start with the answer key. Who labeled it? Did anyone else label the same items, blind, so you know how often the people agree? How many items were contested, and where did they go? And if the vendor changed the model under the same name next month, how would you know?

Glossary

Terms used in this article

AI judge
A language model given an item and a question, and asked to make a call.
Language model
The kind of AI behind tools like ChatGPT and Claude, which writes its answer one word at a time.
Temperature
The setting that controls how much randomness a model uses in choosing each word.
Flip rate
The share of items whose call changed when the same judge was asked again.
Answer key
The set of items labeled by people that the judge is scored against (reference standard).
Stacked gage
Two measurement systems on top of each other: the people who made the key, then the judge.
Human baseline
How often the people who label the items agree with each other.
Contested item
An item the people could not agree on.
Agree bar, catch bar
My pipeline's two pass marks for a judge, one for each kind of error.
Pinned model version
A fixed release of a model, asked for by its exact name, so it cannot change underneath us.
