The $40M rationalization

IT asset management · Fortune 20

2013 – 2019

Layoffs came around most years, so an expense mandate was never abstract. Take cost out of equipment and it does not have to come out of people, which is a mantra I had carried since the budget years and one both sponsoring SVPs shared. Application teams, meanwhile, waited more than seventeen weeks for compute infrastructure and found us hard to transact business with. Nobody had ever mapped the lifecycle end to end. Forensic decomposition found 400 distinct activities, 110 handoffs and 64 interconnected systems split across two SVP organizations, so the seams ran at two scales at once, and around 200 distinct forms of waste sitting inside them, classified against the eight TIMWOODS categories.

Rather than redesigning in isolation, we co-facilitated a Work-Out session that ran from the data center floor to the SVP level, so the people who would have to live in the new model helped build it. Forty-three percent of the critical path turned out to exist because it had always existed, not because it added value. The decomposition method itself was featured at the 2013 and 2014 Verizon Lean Six Sigma conferences.

The target arrived complete rather than negotiated: retire 5,000 legacy servers at roughly $8,000 each in annual machine expense, being hardware and software maintenance plus the licences for whatever was still running on them. Five thousand times eight thousand is the $40M, over five years. The work was finding the two levers that controlled it: how fast an asset could be acquired at the front of the lifecycle, and how fast it could be retired at the back.

Those are less independent than they look. A team gets off end-of-life equipment by migrating onto new equipment, so a seventeen-week provisioning wait was a drag on the retirement side and not only an irritation to the people waiting. I never sized that effect and would not try to now, but it is why the front end mattered to the savings case and not only to the customer.

At the end of the five-year commitment I was asked back to build the mechanism that would confirm it: 5,000 servers out, $40M realized. The data said something better. The rate per server had never moved, so the entire variance was volume: roughly 7,600 servers retired against a target of 5,000, delivering 150% of target at $61M against a $40M case. The instrument built to verify the number is what found the overachievement, which is the argument for measuring your own work rather than reporting it.

What that query really did was close the loop on the redesign. A DMAIC project is meant to end in Control, and most of them end instead when the person who ran it moves on. This one got its Control phase at the five-year mark, from the same person who had run Improve, which is the only reason anybody knows what the redesign was actually worth.

When clarity turned out to be the culture fix

Quality systems · 24×7 technical operations

Early career

A skilled, motivated operations team was losing half its people every year. It was not a pay problem and it was not a culture problem. Nobody had ever documented how the work was supposed to be done, so every technician was guessing their way through mission-critical tasks and carrying the quiet exhaustion of never being sure they were right.

We built an ISO 9000 quality system from the ground up by walking the technicians through their actual daily work: 143 standard operating procedures, process flows, and decision trees for the edge cases, every one refined through their input and made accessible through a knowledge management system inside the operating environment.

Fifteen servers a week to five hundred

Enterprise security migration · 30,000 servers

2013 – 2019

The migration was moving at fifteen servers a week, well short of every executive deadline. The people were working hard; the system they were working inside was the constraint. Data lived in 15 disconnected sources, so pre-qualification failures surfaced during change windows instead of days earlier.

A DMAIC rebuild pulled all 15 sources into a single view of server readiness and re-sequenced the workflow into a pull-based pre-qualify, schedule, implement order, so problems were found and fixed before the window rather than inside it.

Servers that failed pre-qualification were remediated rather than set aside, which fed the ready pool instead of draining it. That made the program harder to run, not easier: two cycles now moved at once, conversion and remediation, and a second cycle nobody can see is a backlog with a nicer name. The data work is what made it affordable. Readiness was inferred by tying configuration logs to operational records, so at any moment the program knew what was not ready, why, and what had just become ready.

That same consolidation surfaced something nobody had asked for. The program had been planned against 20,000 servers. Read together for the first time, the 15 sources showed more than 30,000. Ten thousand servers had been invisible because no single system held all of them.

Carrying that upward was its own work, and most of it landed with the SVP. The scope had grown by half. It was survivable only because throughput had already changed: at fifteen servers a week the discovery would have been unrecoverable, and at five hundred it was a schedule conversation.

The capacity nobody could see

Field workforce capacity · 1,000 technicians

2019 – 2025

A thousand-person field organization was carrying overtime while skills sat idle elsewhere in the same operation, and nobody could see it, because the work area map underneath had grown without governance. It was incomplete, non-contiguous, and redundant: a single ZIP code could appear in six different areas. Dispatch automation was running on geography that did not exist.

I rebuilt the foundation from zero with the field supervisors, who carried the local knowledge no data model could replace, which mountain passes close in winter and which bridge closures break contiguous routing. All 41,326 ZIP codes, contiguous, each one in exactly one area. That 1:1 constraint is what made everything downstream possible. Then I volunteered to build what the clean foundation now allowed: a Force-to-Load capacity model in Python and SQL mapping skill supply against demand by geography and shift.

The phantom cost in the fine print

Vendor contracts · fixed wireless operations

2019 – 2025

Operating costs were climbing faster than output during a high-growth launch. The working assumption was a productivity problem, and a hiring freeze was on the table. The team was working. Output was growing. Nobody could explain the spend.

A forensic review of vendor consumption against contractual terms found the cause in the contracts rather than the teams: volume delivered was running well under the minimum fee thresholds embedded in the agreements. The number everyone was watching had been measuring the wrong thing.

Governing a portfolio without adding headcount

Transformation portfolio governance

2019 – 2025

Portfolio reporting ran on manual reconciliation across disconnected spreadsheets. Every month was a scramble, and forecasts arrived too late to change any decision they were meant to inform.

Making $500M defensible

Corporate FP&A · merger integration

Early career

During a merger integration, roughly $3B of demand pressed against a $2.2B machine expense budget: hardware and software maintenance, software licensing, and the parts that keep data centres running, as distinct from people. That scope belonged to a director and an executive director; I was on the team they relied on for the numbers. The existing process was manual reconciliation with no automated way to produce a forward-looking view merging actuals with estimates, and every synergy claim had to survive audit.

I re-engineered the FP&A process and built the automation behind it, then architected a data integrity governance layer in SQL so the synergy numbers could be defended rather than asserted.

The program finished $500M under budget across six years. Getting there took considerably more than $500M in real reductions, because upward pressure kept arriving and had to be absorbed. The early cloud migration was one of those pressures rather than a source of savings: in the first years it added cost, and that had to be funded out of reductions found elsewhere before any of it could show as an underrun. The reported figure is the net, which is the only version worth defending.

Deciding what not to build

Capital governance · data center operations

Early career

Capital demand arrived faster than it could be evaluated, and everything submitted looked urgent to whoever submitted it. Without a prioritization mechanism, the budget was allocated by whoever asked loudest.

I instituted capital prioritization governance that scored demand against strategic value rather than volume, and designed an application release process alongside it that turned out to have a much longer life than anyone expected.

The release constraint was not the changes themselves, it was the plan each one required. Release managers were authoring every plan from scratch. Standard templates turned that from composition into assembly, and the same team went from roughly 665 releases a year to more than 2,000 without adding anyone. That model was still governing production changes twenty years later.
