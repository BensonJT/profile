Chapter 1

The Leader Carrying the Weight

During the work stoppage in August 2011 I was assigned to a call center and experienced firsthand the complexity of the systems IT had developed over the years. I came home from that assignment with a fresh and deep passion to see those systems work for the people using them.

You may own a piece of the business: P&L accountability, an organization to lead, a budget in the multi-million to billion-dollar range.

And somewhere in your operation, there is friction that has been there long enough to feel normal. Things that should flow don't. Work gets stuck between teams. Systems don't talk to each other. Three people do the same task three different ways. Data lives in disconnected spreadsheets, and nobody can tell you with confidence what is actually happening on the ground right now.

You know it's costing you. But the full picture has been hard to see.

  • Capacity you're paying for but can't use, because skills don't match where the demand is landing.
  • Millions paid for unused contract capacity, because usage was never tracked against minimum fee thresholds.
  • $3M–$4M in avoidable overtime, because the scheduling system can't see where people are or what they can do.
  • Leaders stuck firefighting, managing daily friction instead of executing the strategy they were hired to drive.
  • Talented teams burning out from a lack of clear process. When a high-performing team is struggling, look first at the operational strain they've been asked to carry.

Generic solutions fail in situations like this. Most consultants can either diagnose the problem or build the fix, rarely both. And when they can't do both, you get a process map nobody uses, or a dashboard nobody trusts because it doesn't reflect operational reality.

What operational leaders in this situation need is a partner who can do three things at once: listen with empathy to understand the real pain on the ground, diagnose the root cause with the rigor of a Lean Six Sigma Black Belt, and build the data system that solves it, without waiting six months for IT to deliver something already outdated.

That is the work we do together.

Chapter 2

What Fails, and What Works Instead

Three decades of working inside complex organizations revealed some consistent patterns. Not just about where processes break, but about why well-intentioned solutions fail to stick.

The first pattern: Solutions built without the people who have to live in them don't survive.

Early in my career, I watched technically correct solutions fail because they were designed in a silo. The data was solid. The process map was rigorous. But the operators who were supposed to use the system hadn't shaped it, so they didn't trust it, and it quietly died. That experience locked in the first principle: change management is built into the work from the first day. If the people who feel the friction every day don't see themselves in the solution, the solution will not last.

This is why every engagement starts with listening: conversations with the people carrying the weight, before we build anything.

The second pattern: When visibility is low, the instinct is to add complexity. That almost always makes it worse.

In one engagement, a global operations unit had spent a year adding rules to an auto-dispatch algorithm, and the rules made it worse. The algorithm was fine. The work areas it ran on were fragmented, with a single zip code in six different areas. Rather than debating the diagnosis, we ran a pilot in Virginia, and when the auto-dispatch rate improved, the data made the case for a national rollout. The full story is in Chapter 4.

The lesson I took from it: when you see something others don't yet see, stop arguing and build the pilot.

The third pattern: Progress that works today beats perfection that arrives too late.

The tools in this work, Python, SQL, AI-assisted development, exist to solve the problem in front of us, not to produce elegant code for its own sake. An "inefficient" script that runs overnight and gives a team visibility they didn't have before is more valuable than a technically pristine system still in planning six months from now. The discipline is knowing what's good enough to move the organization forward, and what needs more work before it goes into production.

This is the orientation that drives the work: operational leaders deserve solutions they can use now, built to scale as their needs evolve.

Chapter 3

The Collaborative Process

Here is how we move from "we have this friction problem" to "the system is running and the team owns it."

Step 1  ·  Weeks 1–4

Listen, Map, and Align

This is where most engagements rush. We don't.

The first weeks are dedicated to building shared understanding. That means conversations with everyone who touches the process, not just leadership looking at KPIs, but the operators and supervisors feeling the friction daily. Where does it hurt? What are you carrying that you shouldn't have to carry? What workarounds has the team built because the official system doesn't work?

At the same time, functional process decomposition is underway, a method combining SIPOC and IDEF0 analysis that maps what is actually happening step by step, not what the three-year-old PowerPoint says is happening. This goes into a detailed working document that becomes a shared artifact the team can react to and correct.

We also identify what is being measured (if anything), what data exists, and where it lives. We secure read-only access so we can understand what the data looks like and what it means.

By the end of this phase: the friction is mapped, the people involved trust that they were heard, and we have the data access to diagnose the root cause.

Step 2  ·  Weeks 5–16+

Restore the Foundation

You cannot model complexity on a broken foundation. If the underlying data structure is fragmented, building analytics on top of it amplifies the problem. This phase addresses what is broken before anything new is built.

In the zip code engagement described in Chapter 2, this phase took two and a half months: 41,326 zip codes rationalized into 521 work areas across 50 territories. We did that work with every field supervisor, because they had local knowledge that no data model can replace. They knew which rivers, bridges and mountain passes split areas that looked contiguous on a map, and which boundaries looked clean on paper but created chaos in the field. Their input turned a technically correct map into one the teams trusted, because they helped build it.

At the end of that phase we gave one team sole authority over work area definitions, so the foundation stayed clean.

This step is scope-dependent. It could take six weeks. It could take twelve or more. But it is non-negotiable. Every solution built on a fragile foundation eventually inherits that fragility.

Step 3  ·  Weeks 17–28

Build, Show, Listen, Adapt

This is where the actual infrastructure takes shape, data pipelines, workforce planning models, dashboards, automated reporting. Whatever the specific problem demands.

The build is iterative. I build a first version and bring it back to the team. And something useful happens at this point: because they can now see the data clearly for the first time, the team starts asking questions they couldn't have articulated before the build started. "Can we filter this by shift?" "Is there a way to flag when capacity drops below a threshold?" "What does this look like by geography?"

Some of those questions are a new query. Some require new dimensions in the pipeline. Either way, the model adapts. This feedback loop continues until the system is answering the questions that matter to how the operation is run, not just the questions we thought we had at the start.

The goal at the end of this phase is infrastructure that keeps running after the analysis is done.

Step 4  ·  Customized

Stewardship and Handoff

The goal is your team's independence. The handoff is structured around your organization's capacity and culture:

  • Retainer Support: Ongoing part-time stewardship for clients without internal technical capacity, or where the model is mission-critical and continuity matters.
  • Internal Transition: Full documentation, architecture, data flow diagrams, maintenance playbook, plus structured training so your IT or data team owns and runs the system.
  • Hybrid: Internal transition plus a 3–6 month support window where complex questions or updates are handled during the learning curve.

We choose the right path during the build phase, not at the end. The measure of a successful engagement is that the solution continues to deliver value long after the engagement closes.

Chapter 4

Selected Outcomes

I delivered each of these working closely with leadership and their operational teams.

Auto-Dispatch From 30–40% to 70–80%

A Global Network Operations unit had been struggling with their auto-dispatch tool for a year. The algorithm itself wasn't the problem. But field teams had stopped trusting it, and leadership was frustrated by the lack of progress despite repeated fixes.

The Friction

Any manager could create a work area or assign a zip code, so years of drift left single zip codes in six different work areas and a technician in Baltimore eligible for work in Charlottesville. The map covered only about 30,000 of the country's 41,326 zip codes and could never serve the rest. Every rule added to "fix" performance was compensating for broken data.

Our Action

I rebuilt the zip code universe from US Postal Service files cross-referenced against internal systems. Then, working alongside every field supervisor, we redesigned 41,326 zip codes into 521 contiguous work areas built around rivers, bridges and mountain passes. We gave one team sole authority over work area definitions so the map stayed stable. On that foundation, I built a Python/SQL Force-to-Load workforce capacity model that mapped supply (people, skills, geography, schedule) against demand, with PERT-based time pricing for each job type.

Selected Outcomes
  • $18M–$22M in hidden workforce capacity surfaced, with a training plan for each technician to recover it
  • 50 additional Building Engineers approved on a data-backed business case
  • $3M–$4M in avoidable overtime cut by aligning schedules to demand by day, hour and shift
  • Auto-dispatch success rate moved from the 30–40% range to the 70–80% range nationally, from the work area redesign
  • $1.5M saved by building the capacity model in house, $1.5M less than the alternative

Fixing the map did what a year of new rules could not: auto-dispatch went from the 30–40% range to 70–80%.

Attrition From 50% to 1%

A 24x7 Technical Support and Operations environment had a talent problem that wasn't a talent problem. The team was skilled. But they were being asked to guess their way through mission-critical tasks because nobody had documented how the work was supposed to be done.

Half the team left every year. Morale was low, and nobody could say exactly why. Talented people were carrying weight they shouldn't have to carry.

The Friction

No documented standards. No shared operating procedures. Each technician navigating complex, high-stakes tasks differently, without confidence that their approach was right. Procedural errors and critical outages were high, and the cause was ambiguity built into the system.

Our Action

Working alongside the team, we built an ISO 9000 Quality Management System from the ground up. 143 standard operating procedures, developed by walking the technicians through their actual daily work, process flows, if-then-else logic, decision trees for edge cases. Every document reflected what they actually did, refined through their input. I built the knowledge management system and an intranet site that held all 143 SOPs under document control, so the 24x7 teams could reach them mid-shift. The quality system passed its ISO 9000-2000 registration and re-registration audits, internal and external.

Selected Outcomes
  • Procedural errors reduced 86%
  • Critical outages fell 80%
  • Attrition dropped from 50% to 1%
  • All availability targets met for the first time in organizational history

Once the team knew how the work was supposed to be done, attrition fell from 50% to 1%.

The $40M Rationalization

Layoffs came around most years, so an enterprise mandate for expense reduction was never abstract. Take cost out of equipment and it does not have to come out of people, a mantra I had lived by since the budget years and one both sponsoring SVPs shared. In a large, complex IT environment the room to do that was real, because the waste was in the process itself.

The Friction

An end-to-end IT asset lifecycle that kept application teams waiting more than 17 weeks for infrastructure. Forensic decomposition found 400 distinct activities across 64 systems in two weeks, and around 200 distinct forms of waste, classified against the eight TIMWOODS categories. Most of the delay was in the 110 handoffs between owners. A critical path where 43% of the steps were unnecessary, work that existed because it had always existed, not because it added value.

Our Action

Rather than redesigning in isolation, we co-facilitated a large-scale Work Out session spanning from the data center floor to the SVP level. Every stakeholder who touched the process was in the room. The goal was to build the new model together, with the people who would have to live in it. Together we deconstructed the old model, identified the vital few steps and institutionalized a new governance structure across the program. The session produced seven major multi-year solutions, and the SVPs approved all seven.

Selected Outcomes
  • $40M five-year savings case, set at project start as 5,000 legacy servers at roughly $8K each in annual machine expense
  • $61M realized against the $40M case, 150% of target. The rate per server never moved, so the whole variance was volume: roughly 7,600 servers retired against a target of 5,000, established five years on by the SQL analysis built to verify it
  • Asset acquisition velocity improved 82%, from 17+ weeks down to under 3 weeks
  • Decommissioning cycle time cut 37%, from 46 days to 29

Counting decommissioned systems in the asset database five years on closed the loop on the redesign itself. A DMAIC project is meant to end in Control, and most end when the person who ran it moves on. This one got its Control phase five years late, from the same person who ran the Improve phase.

Two weeks of decomposition found the 43% of the critical path that nobody needed.

From 15 Servers a Week to 500

A Fortune 20 enterprise security migration was stuck. The program was scoped at 20,000 Linux servers, and the team was processing 15 per week. At that pace, every executive deadline was unreachable.

Six people ran the program, and two system administrators did every migration. They were working hard. The system they were working inside was the problem.

The Friction

Manual workflows with no visibility into server readiness created a cycle of discovery failures during change windows. Conversions turned into hours of circular troubleshooting inside night change windows. Pre-qualification issues that could have been caught days earlier were surfacing at implementation time. And because data lived in 15 disconnected sources, nobody had a current view of where things stood.

Our Action

Co-leading with the program's lead PM, I ran a DMAIC transformation to rebuild the foundation for flow. I built an automated management information system that pulled 15 disparate data sources into one master data set and scored every server nightly against 13 readiness checks. We re-engineered the workflow into a pull-based Pre-Qualify → Schedule → Implement sequence so that problems were found and resolved before the change window, not during it. We borrowed Scrum practices, including daily standups for status and obstacle removal, run on the near-real-time readiness data.

Servers that failed pre-qualification were remediated rather than set aside, which fed the ready pool instead of draining it. That deliberately made the program harder to run: two cycles now moved at once, conversion and remediation. Adding a parallel stream is the wrong call without visibility and the right one with it, and the visibility is what the data work bought (readiness inferred by tying configuration logs to operational records), so the program always knew what was not ready, why, and what had just become ready.

Then the data said something nobody had asked it. Combined and interpreted together for the first time, those 15 sources showed more than 30,000 servers in scope, not 20,000. Ten thousand had been invisible because no single system held all of them, and the plan had been measuring the wrong denominator from the beginning.

Escalating that was its own work, most of it in conversations with the sponsoring SVP. Telling an executive the program just grew by half is hard news. It was survivable only because throughput had already changed: at 15 servers a week the discovery would have been unrecoverable, and at 500 it was a schedule conversation. The measurement system paid for itself before it ever produced a status report.

Selected Outcomes
  • 15 to 319 servers per week averaged across Q4, a 21× increase on the starting rate
  • Over 500 per week sustained at Q4 exit, 33× the starting rate. The quarterly average understates it because it includes the ramp
  • 15 integrated data sources turned status reporting into operational orchestration
  • 10,000+ additional servers surfaced, growing the known scope by half
  • Program recognized at SVP level for delivery pace

The same two system administrators went from 15 servers a week to more than 500.

$500M Under a $2.2B Budget

During the MCI merger integration, roughly $3B of demand pressed against a $2.2B machine expense budget, and leadership needed to know at any moment where actuals stood against it across a six-year program. The existing workflow was a manual reconciliation process, disconnected spreadsheets, no automated way to generate forward-looking "Best View" forecasts that merged actuals with estimates.

Producing the same output by hand would have taken four and a half times the staff just to keep pace with monthly reporting cycles.

The Friction

A highly manual financial governance process that made accurate, timely reporting nearly impossible at scale. Every month was a scramble. Every forecast required hours of manual reconciliation that left the team with little time to analyze what the numbers meant.

Our Action

I reengineered the FP&A cycle with DMAIC and built the tooling in Access SQL, macros and Excel. One view reconciled the fixed budget, the daily-moving "Best View" forecast and monthly actuals. An Access front end let every analyst report the budget impact of their own vendor changes. Vendors that appeared under as many as a hundred spellings in SAP and PeopleSoft were collapsed into single entities, so spend could be aggregated and negotiated.

Selected Outcomes
  • $500M under budget across six years on hardware, software and maintenance contracts, net of the upward pressure absorbed along the way, so reaching it took considerably more than $500M in real reductions
  • An estimated $4.2M in manual labor avoided through automation, output that would have taken 4.5x the staff by hand
  • FP&A cycle time cut 70%, and budget-versus-actual reconciliation cut from 3 days of hand-checking to 4 hours
  • A $2.2B budget forecast and tracked against actuals across six years

The tooling did what 4.5 times the staff would have done by hand, every month for six years.

Half the Run Rate in Contract Minimums

In 2025, fixed wireless install volumes doubled and vendor spend was headed over budget. Leadership was prepared to go over. Before they did, I analyzed how much of that spend was tied up in contract minimum fees and alerted them.

The Friction

Vendors billed against minimum fees that bought a fixed number of truck rolls, at a different rate once the minimum was met. Sales projections arrived as spreadsheets on their own schedule. Nobody had connected the projections, the minimums and the budget, so the budget was an estimate defended by argument.

Our Action

Working alongside the finance and operations teams, I built a five-year truck roll forecast that tied sales projections to the minimum fees in each vendor contract. With it I showed leadership the financial projections for 20 different scenarios. Most still came in over budget, because they left the cost structure unchanged. The few that fixed the run rate all required new cost structure actions. The cost driver was minimum fees, not truck rolls, and the root cause was an earlier decision to move markets from lower-cost vendors to the highest-cost one.

Selected Outcomes
  • Root cause identified: minimum fees, not truck rolls, after markets moved from lower-cost vendors to the highest-cost one
  • $15M of a $30M run rate shown early in the year to be non-working spend, bought by contract minimums the arriving volume never used
  • 20 budget scenarios modeled, and only the few with new cost structure actions fixed the run rate
  • A 2026 pathway worth $2M from a vendor price adjustment alone, scaling toward $10M by moving the lowest-volume markets off the highest-cost vendor, with the data to back it

Half the run rate was paying for truck rolls that never happened.

Chapter 5

Is This the Right Partnership?

This work is for operational leaders who own a piece of the business and know the friction is costing them time, money, and morale.

We are likely a good fit if:

  • You are ready to look at uncomfortable data. Real improvement starts with visibility into what is happening, even when the picture is complex.
  • You will involve your team. The people who feel the friction every day need to help shape the solution. A top-down fix without their input rarely lasts.
  • You are open to fixing the foundation first. The most durable results come from solving the root cause before speeding anything up. Surfacing $18M–$22M of hidden capacity required months of foundation work before the model could be built.
  • You value impact over optics. The goal is an operation that runs better, with the numbers to prove it.
  • You are organizationally ready: real ownership of the problem, access to the relevant data, infrastructure to build on, and the willingness to act on what the data surfaces.

This may not be the right fit if:

  • You need a cosmetic improvement to show progress by next quarter. This work takes time to do right. Quick wins exist, but sustainable transformation requires patience with the foundation phase.
  • A top-down fix without team involvement is the only option available. Lasting systems require the people who live in them to help build them.
  • You want a tool to install and walk away from. This work is collaborative and iterative. The systems that last are built with the people who run them.

The best engagements happen when leaders come ready to look at the full picture, even when it is complex, even when some of what it reveals is uncomfortable. If that describes how you operate, we will work well together.
