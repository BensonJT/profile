About

How I work.

Near the start of my career I wrote procedures for a 24×7 operations team that was losing half its people every year. Not because they were bad at the job, but because nobody had ever told them how the job was supposed to be done. When we finished writing it down, attrition went from 50% to 1%.

That taught me the thing I have been doing ever since. Behind most broken processes is a person carrying weight they should not have to carry, and the weight is almost always structural rather than personal.

So the work is always the same shape. Listen first, to the people actually doing it, before anything gets designed. Take the process apart until you can see where the value is leaking. Quantify it, so the case makes itself rather than needing to be argued. Then build something the team can run without me.

That last part matters most, and it is the part that is easiest to skip. A model nobody owns is a model that dies the quarter after I leave.

I discovered early in my career what made me want to get up and go to work. It was the chance to leave a fingerprint on the business: a team, and the process they run, measurably more effective and efficient than when I joined them.

One thing worth saying plainly, since it usually comes up. The ideas, the judgment, and the standards are mine. AI helps me articulate and build faster than I could alone.

I am not a software engineer and I am not going to pretend to be one. I learned data engineering the long way, self-taught, over about fifteen years, and I have written a great deal of production SQL and Python. What I did not do is become an architect. So on Forester and Meridian I bring the problem, the method, and the judgment about whether the answer is right.

That division of labor is not a caveat. It is why one person can now ship what used to take a team.

Leadership

Six operating principles, earned rather than claimed.

These are not aspirations. Each one is a behavior, and the colleagues below put each one on the record independently.

Lead with empathy

Understand what someone carries before diagnosing their performance

01

Listen before diagnosing. Name the pressure before proposing a fix. Hold space when it is uncertain. Invite challenge and then actually incorporate the pushback. Empathy is not softness; it is diagnostic discipline applied to people.

“Jeff is approachable, he listens to the team members, and pivots when necessary to implement their recommendations.”

Carolyn DeReus EAM program team member, Verizon

Give people the context you would give your best prompt

Context determines decision quality

02

Explain the why before the what. Share constraints, not just decisions. Name what you do not know. Make the business case visible to the team and the backlog visible to stakeholders, including the prioritization logic. Deliver honest sequencing even when it disappoints.

“His process maps provided clarity to roles and responsibilities through reorganizations allowing team members to understand how a process flows, where they fit in, and which steps need to be followed to eliminate duplication of efforts between teams.”

John V. Demetrio Director, Verizon IT Network Implementation

Diagnose before you design

What appears to be the problem is rarely the actual problem

03

Map current state before proposing future state. Separate symptoms from root causes. Interview the operators before designing the process. Hold the hypothesis loosely. Solving the real problem produces what the apparent fix never would.

“Jeffrey has always been and remains the person to turn to when there is a problem or effort so entangled that most do not know where to start…”

Sally Curran Verizon

Remove friction

Automate the drag that keeps talented people from their real work

04

When people have the tools and the clarity to do the work well, they stay. Most attrition that gets diagnosed as a culture problem is an ambiguity problem wearing a disguise.

“Jeff’s gift for critical thinking, process improvement and attention to detail helped us to automate a task that took 3 days of manual ‘stare & compare’ into a mouse click that compiled the data in less than 5 minutes.”

Collin Ice Verizon FP&A colleague

Invest in growth

Measure success by whether the people around you got stronger

05

The team that works alongside you should be measurably stronger for it. Build capability, not dependency. Eight Green Belts through full certification, a four-person analytics team brought onto AI-assisted development, and a hundred and forty-three procedures written with the technicians rather than for them.

“When I had the pleasure of working with Jeff, I referred to him as ‘the tide that lifts all ships’ due to his support for teammates and investment in our organization’s internal data and processes.”

Jeff Parker Strategy, Transformation & Innovation Leader at Verizon; managed Jeffrey directly

Measure what changes

Improvement claimed without a baseline is just a story

06

No baseline, no claim. The delta is the proof, and it also ensures the people who did the work get the credit they earned. This one applies to my own work too: on the current client engagement I built a vectorization approach, measured it 3.3× slower than what it replaced, reverted it, and recorded it as a closed question rather than quietly dropping it.

“Crucially, this model also became the backbone for our headcount modeling and reporting — providing the critical reporting to build robust, data-backed business cases for both requesting additional headcount and justifying workforce reductions or reallocations during periods of changing demand.”

Kristine Reelfs Global Network Operations, Verizon; her team used the force-to-load model daily

“Lead with empathy. Give your team and your stakeholders the context they deserve. Diagnose before you design. Remove friction. Invest in growth. Measure what changes. Do those six things consistently, and the results take care of themselves.”

Jeffrey Benson
