The Mathematics of Morale: Calibrating Load to Lift

Jeffrey Benson  ·  2023-2025

Capacity Series  ·  Part A  |  Part B

Moving beyond averages to find the hidden capacity in your roster.

This builds on the "You Can't Automate a Mess" case study: once the map is trustworthy, Force-to-Load turns it into a capacity model leaders can act on.

One of the loneliest positions for a leader is standing between a Finance team asking, "Why is this costing so much?" and an Operations team saying, "We are drowning."

I saw that gap at Verizon in Global Network Operations (GNO). Capacity planning ran on averages, so Finance and Operations argued about staffing from two different realities. Once we had fixed the work area map in 2023, I volunteered to build a model that would give them one: Force-to-Load, which started at the end of 2023 and went through four major iterations through 2025.

I built it in Python and SQL, in house, for $1.5M less than IT had asked to build it. Then we refined it with the team that used it every day. Each version answered the question in front of us, and daily use kept surfacing what it still couldn't see. Along the way, using Gemini to help optimize the code, I cut the runtime 25% (from about four hours to three), so we could run what-if scenarios while leaders were still in the room. This article walks through how the model works.

The Friction

Often the organization has no shared language to reconcile those two realities. A company might rely on legacy spreadsheets that average "headcount" against "ticket volume," and the averages hide what the team is struggling with.

A spreadsheet doesn't know that your technicians in one region face a two-hour commute between sites, or that a team is bogged down by bad data from tickets left open over a weekend.

To move from operational strain to sustainable performance, we have to replace broad assumptions with precise measures. That means building a Workforce Capacity Model, the Force-to-Load (F2L).

When I build one of these models with a leader, I focus on bringing three components together: The Supply, The Demand and The Pricing.

Pillar I

Supply, The Human Reality

Supply is often reduced to a count of "FTEs." A generic FTE can't tell you who can do a specific job, in a specific place, on a specific shift. To measure supply accurately, we start from the roster as it stands and build a Dynamic Capability Map in place of a static list:

  • The People: who is on the team today.
  • The Skills: what are they certified to handle? (This is different from their job title.)
  • The Geography: where are they dispatched, and where do they live?
  • The Schedule: the shifts they work and when they're available.

By mapping this backward (what did we have?) and forward (what will we have?), we turn "Supply" from a static number into a live view of capability.

Pillar II

Demand, The Signal

Demand is the work to be done. To model it, we break each request down into what it really takes:

  • Skills Required: connecting the request to the specific capability needed.
  • Disposition: is the work open, closed or pending?
  • The Time Tax: setup time, commute time and execution time.
If we leave out the travel or the setup, the model describes a job nobody actually does.

Pillar III

Pricing, The Time-Cost

This is where the model moves from reporting to insight. In this model, a task's price is measured in minutes.

Standard models often fail here because they rely on simple means (averages), which get skewed by outliers like a ticket accidentally left open for 400 hours, or one closed with 0 minutes.

To find the "True Price" of a task and protect the team from that skew, we use a Modified PERT Formula (Program Evaluation and Review Technique):

Modified PERT Formula

Price (min) = (P₂₀ + P₈₀ + (4 × P₅₀)) ÷ 6

  • P₂₀ (20th Percentile): the fast, easy runs.
  • P₈₀ (80th Percentile): the complex, difficult runs.
  • P₅₀ (Median): the standard, normal run.

Why this matters: by weighting the median (×4) and capping the extremes at the 20th and 80th percentiles, we arrive at a fair, defensible standard. We don't expect teams to work at the speed of the fastest outlier, and we don't pad the numbers with data errors.

IV. The Outcome

Once the model calculates in minutes, translating it to dollars is simple algebra. At GNO, the model covered a 1,000-person field force across the domestic United States, and it surfaced opportunities the averages had hidden:

  • The Insight: the model revealed an $18M to $22M capacity variance.
  • The Cause: skill mismatch, and the answer was training the people we had. Leadership also used the model to justify adding heads, and when force reductions were dictated from above, to decide where to target them. It was never used to recommend a reduction.
  • The Reality: we were paying for capacity "on the clock" that didn't line up with the incoming demand. Modeling demand by day, hour and shift let GNO align schedules to peak demand and cut $3M to $4M in avoidable overtime.
  • The Shortfalls: pricing work in minutes showed leadership the geographies with sustained supply shortfalls, and that won approval for 50 additional Building Engineers.

Without the F2L model, that opportunity stayed invisible inside the averages.

From Insight to Action

Identifying the value is only half the job.

We took the $18M to $22M variance past a number on a slide. We showed leadership exactly which technicians in which geographies needed to cross-train in which specific skills, and turned it into a customized training plan for each tech.

That moved us from a high-level finding to an execution plan, a roster of names and training requirements that GNO could use to add supply where skills were short.

Beyond Efficiency: The Dignity Dimension

This is about dignity as much as efficiency.

When a model is accurate, teams feel seen because their actual workload shows up in the data. When the data is accurate, executives feel confident because they're making decisions on what is really happening.

The Takeaway

Averages hide friction, and they hide opportunity.

When capacity planning is built on "headcount vs. volume" averages, Finance and Operations end up debating two different realities. Force-to-Load gives them a shared language (supply, demand and pricing in minutes), so decisions are grounded in what the work actually costs the team to perform.

From insight to action: I don't stop at identifying the variance. The deliverable is an execution-ready plan that shows which skills are missing where and turns that into a training plan (often down to specific geographies and, when appropriate, a roster-level plan by name and skill) so leaders can close the shortfalls instead of only discussing them.
