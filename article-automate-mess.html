Operations · Automation

You Can't Automate a Mess.

Jeffrey Benson  ·  2023-2025

Capacity Series  ·  Part A  |  Part B

How redesigning the zip code map under an automated dispatch tool restored the field's trust, moved auto-dispatch from the 30-40% range to 70-80%, and laid the foundation for a capacity model that surfaced $18M to $22M in capacity variance.

This case study comes from large-scale field operations, but the same pattern shows up anywhere automation is being asked to make up for bad data, weak governance or a broken process.

The "Smarter Algorithm" Trap

When automation fails in field operations, the first instinct is to make the algorithm "smarter." We add more logic layers, more rules and more code.

I watched this happen at Verizon in 2023, in Global Network Operations (GNO). The business had set an objective of 85% touchless dispatching, and the automated dispatch tool (the "Autoloader") was nowhere near it. For months the business and IT teams had tried to improve its performance by adding more complex rule sets. The needle barely moved, and as the rules got more complicated, the Autoloader's success rate often went down.

The field didn't trust the tool, IT was frustrated, and leadership expected results.

So instead of adding another rule, I looked underneath the tool at the data it was working from: the map of which technicians covered which zip codes.

Act I

The Governance Vacuum

The algorithm was doing what it was told. The problem was the map, a condition we came to call "Zip Code Anarchy."

No single team governed the map, so the definition of a "Work Area" had drifted for years. Any manager could create a work area or assign a zip code to one. When I rebuilt the zip code universe from US Postal Service files and cross-referenced it against our internal systems, the dispatch map covered only about 30,000 of the country's 41,326 zip codes. The Autoloader could never serve the rest. The zips it did cover had their own problems:

  • The "Six-Assignment" Error: a single zip code assigned to six different legacy work areas.
  • The "Ghost" Geography: zip codes assigned, through data entry mistakes, to work areas in entirely different states.

The Autoloader followed that data faithfully. It would send a technician hours away because the map said the job was "in their territory." IT pushed back at first on cleaning up the map, believing the algorithm could handle the noise. As operators, we knew it couldn't, so we piloted the redesign in Virginia first to prove it.

The Fix: Redesign the Map With the Field

I realized we could not build a credible capacity model (what became Force-to-Load) until we fixed the map. That took two things: a forensic redesign of the work areas and a partnership with the field to make them stick.

Step 1

The "Strawman" Design

Partnering with the Capacity Management team, I led the draft designs for entire states. We kept zip codes contiguous and drew the boundaries around rivers, bridges, mountain passes and traffic patterns, and we made sure every zip code lived in exactly one home. I also worked out the order the dispatcher loaded work in and sized the metro zones by volume, making technicians primary in a few zones and secondary in others, so the work balanced across the field instead of piling onto one tech at a time.

Step 2

The Field Sessions

We co-authored the map with the people who knew the ground. We held working sessions with supervisors and technicians and walked them through our drafts.

  • The "Why": we explained that contiguous areas meant less drive time and fewer illogical Autoloader assignments. We framed it around shorter drive times.
  • The "How": we adjusted the boundaries based on their local knowledge. A supervisor would tell us a mountain pass closed in winter, so those zips couldn't share a work area.

This was change management as much as data entry. Once the Virginia pilot proved it, we rolled the redesign out nationally with every field supervisor: 41,326 zip codes restructured into 521 work areas across 50 territories in two and a half months. By the time the map was final, the field had already approved it.

Step 3

The Governance Lock

Once the redesign was complete, we set a new rule: only the Capacity Management team had the authority to change a work area. That made the corrected map a maintained source of truth instead of a one-time cleanup, and the era of "Zip Code Anarchy" was over.

The Micro-Win: The Autoloader Started Working

The impact was immediate. Before the redesign, the Autoloader was placing work automatically in the 30 to 40 percent range. Once the new map was in place nationally (no overlaps, logical boundaries), it ran in the 70 to 80 percent range.

After months of trying to code our way out of the problem, we proved that a smarter algorithm needs better data first.

Act II

The Force-to-Load Model (The Big Win)

With 521 well-governed work areas underneath us, we could finally build the capacity model I had volunteered to build, starting at the end of 2023: a Force-to-Load (F2L) Capacity Model covering a 1,000-person field force across the domestic United States.

Capacity planning had run on averages, so Finance and Operations argued about staffing from two different realities. F2L gave them one.

  • The Architecture: I built the model in Python and SQL, using the new work areas as its geography, and priced work in minutes with a modified PERT distribution instead of averages.
  • The Speed: we took the model through four major iterations through 2025 with the team that used it every day. Using Gemini to help optimize the code, I cut the runtime 25% (from about four hours to three), which made "what-if" scenario planning practical.

The Outcome

The project began as a way to fix a broken dispatch tool. Because we fixed the map first, it ended as a financial win.

  • $18M to $22M in Capacity Variance Surfaced: by laying supply and demand over the new work areas, we saw that many "shortages" were really skill shortages in specific work areas. We turned the variance into a customized training plan for each tech, so GNO could add supply where the skills were short.
  • Approval for 50 Additional Building Engineers: pricing work in minutes showed leadership the geographies with sustained supply shortfalls, which gave them the data to justify headcount where the demand actually was.
  • $3M to $4M Less in Avoidable Overtime: we modeled demand by day, hour and shift so GNO could align technician schedules to peak demand.
  • $1.5M Less Than the Alternative: we built the model in house for $1.5M less than IT had asked to build it.

The Takeaway

You cannot build a skyscraper on a swamp.

Many organizations try to put advanced AI or capacity models on top of legacy data nobody has checked, and the automation only makes the mess move faster. I believe you have to be an Operator (someone who understands the pain of the field) before you can be an Architect (someone who builds the model to fix it).
